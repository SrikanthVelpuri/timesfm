Base price as a strategic layer — where TimesFM stops and the bot begins

TimesFM produces a demand-driven daily-fare forecast. The base price is a different object: a strategic anchor that the dynamic-pricing bot deviates from on each customer request. As bots take over more of the per-customer decision, the base price becomes the controllable strategic surface — the guardrail the bot operates inside. This page makes that layer explicit and shows where TimesFM stops.

TL;DR. Pricing is four layers: (1) Forecast (TimesFM 2.5 + XReg + LoRA), (2) Elasticity / response (GBM + hierarchical Bayes), (3) Base-price optimizer (MIP + Bayesian opt), (4) Dynamic-pricing bot (rules + contextual bandits). The portfolio today owns layer 1; layers 2–4 are owned by Operations Research, Pricing Strategy, and Pricing Tech. As bots eat the dynamic loop, the base price becomes more important, not less — the bot is good at execution; the base price decides what is being executed.

The four-layer pricing stack

Each layer has a distinct output, owner, model class, and update cadence. Mixing layers is the most common failure I've seen in airline RM — usually a forecast team trying to set policy, or a pricing team treating a forecast coefficient as a causal elasticity. Keep them separate.

1 · Forecast layer

  • Model: TimesFM 2.5 + XReg head + per-family LoRA.
  • Output: q10 / q50 / q90 daily fare, 30-day horizon, per OD-pair, per cabin.
  • Owner: Applied Science (us).
  • Cadence: nightly + on-demand (off-cycle re-score).
  • What it answers: “What will the realised average fare look like under current policy?”

2 · Elasticity / response layer

  • Model options: LightGBM + random effects (GPBoost), hierarchical Bayes (Stan/PyMC), Double ML, DeepIV, causal forests.
  • Output: ∂E[demand]/∂Price + cross-elasticities, per (route family, cabin, days-to-departure, segment).
  • Owner: Operations Research.
  • Cadence: weekly.
  • What it answers: “If we move price by Δ, how much does demand move?”

3 · Base-price optimizer

  • Model options: mixed-integer programming, Bayesian optimization, simulation-based grid search.
  • Output: anchor fare, fare-ladder rungs, floor, ceiling, per (OD, cabin, season).
  • Owner: Pricing Strategy.
  • Cadence: weekly to monthly.
  • What it answers: “What is the published anchor that maximises expected revenue under constraints?”

4 · Dynamic-pricing bot

  • Model options: contextual bandits (Thompson sampling), business-rules engine, lightweight elasticity-step lookup.
  • Output: per-request fare = base × f(load, days-to-departure, competitor, channel).
  • Owner: Pricing Tech.
  • Cadence: per-request, milliseconds.
  • What it answers: “What price do we show this specific shopper right now?”

Why base price matters MORE when bots run the dynamic loop

The intuition cuts the other way for most people: “if the bot is doing the pricing, why bother with a base price at all?” Three reasons.

  1. Bots execute within a strategy. Without an anchor, the bot has nothing to deviate from. Garbage-in for the bot if the base is wrong — the bot can only adjust around what it's given.
  2. The bot's gain is a thin layer of personalisation on top of the base. A typical bandit picks up 0.5–2% of revenue from segment-specific deviation. A $20 wrong base swamps a $2 right personalisation.
  3. Auditability and antitrust. The published base price is what regulators see; the bot deviation is bounded by the floor/ceiling guardrails. The base price is the visible strategic statement; the bot is the controlled execution.

The user's example is the cleanest illustration:

The $150 vs $220 problem. The market supports a base of $220. Pricing files $150 by mistake. The bot detects strong demand and lifts 20%: $150 → $180. The bot reacted correctly. The market would have supported $220 → $260. The bot was right; the starting point was wrong; the airline left $80 of revenue per booked seat on the table because the strategy was misfiled, not because the execution was bad. Bots optimise the deviation; the base price decides what is being deviated from.

One-liner: Base price = strategy. Bot = execution. Bots are good at the second and have nothing to say about the first.

The contract between layers

Each layer hands the next a small, versioned, signed JSON. This is what the contracts look like in production:

# Layer 1 (TimesFM) -> Layer 2 (Elasticity)
{ "od":"DFW-LAX", "cabin":"main", "horizon_d":30,
  "fare_q10":[...30 floats], "fare_q50":[...], "fare_q90":[...],
  "covariate_snapshot_hash":"sha256:...", "model_tag":"aa-timesfm-xreg-v1.4" }

# Layer 2 (Elasticity) -> Layer 3 (Base-price optimizer)
{ "od":"DFW-LAX", "season":"Q4", "cabin":"main",
  "elasticity":-1.21, "elasticity_se":0.08,
  "cross":{"DFW-DAL":-0.04,"DFW-BUR":-0.07},
  "method":"DoubleML+GPBoost", "fit_date":"2026-04-05" }

# Layer 3 (Base-price optimizer) -> Layer 4 (Bot)
{ "od":"DFW-LAX", "anchor_fare":312, "floor":248, "ceiling":520,
  "ladder":[248,272,294,312,348,398,460,520],
  "approval":"director-signed-2026-04-08" }

# Layer 4 (Bot) -> Booking front-end
{ "od":"DFW-LAX", "request_id":"...", "fare":318,
  "rationale":{"base":312,"load_adj":+4,"competitor_adj":+2,"channel_adj":0} }

Two things worth noting. First, every contract carries the producer's model tag and an approval/audit field — cheap insurance for legal review. Second, the bot's output explains itself in terms of the base; this is what makes the deviation bounded and auditable.

Where TimesFM stops

What TimesFM does well in this stack

  • Multi-step calibrated quantile forecasts (q10/q50/q90 over 30 days).
  • Cross-route information transfer through the frozen trunk.
  • Cold-start on new OD-pairs via zero-shot.
  • Native ingestion of dynamic + static covariates through the XReg head.
  • Cheap retraining ($7/quarter) keeps the forecast fresh.

What TimesFM does NOT do

  • Estimate causal elasticity from observational price/quantity data — no confounding control built in.
  • Optimise a price subject to constraints (floor, ceiling, ladder smoothness).
  • Predict competitor reaction in equilibrium (it treats competitor fare as exogenous).
  • Learn from a deployed-policy reward signal (no closed-loop training; that would be policy-leakage).
  • Decide the strategic anchor — that's a policy choice, not a forecast.
Right tool, wrong scope. TimesFM is the demand-side forecaster; the rest of the stack solves problems with different assumptions and different math. Calling TimesFM a “pricing model” is wrong both technically and operationally; calling it the “forecast layer underneath the pricing stack” is right.

The single most important arrow is the one that doesn't exist: realised bookings feed L2 (elasticity refit) but not L1 (forecast). A forecast that trains on its own deployed-policy outcomes contracts toward the policy you ran — you stop seeing the demand curve and start seeing the conversion funnel.

Antitrust separation gets sharper, not looser

The four-layer split is also the cleanest answer to the antitrust question. Each layer is a different kind of artifact under regulatory review:

  • Forecast (L1) — a probabilistic statement about demand. Auditable from the data hash + model tag.
  • Elasticity (L2) — an estimated causal parameter. Auditable from the estimator class + identification strategy (instrument, randomisation, control set).
  • Base price (L3) — a policy decision. Auditable from the optimizer's constraint set + Director-level approval signature.
  • Bot deviation (L4) — a bounded execution. Auditable from the bandit's exploration log + the floor/ceiling that bounds it.

Compliance reviews each layer separately. The page does not move the boundary between Applied Science and Pricing Strategy; it explains it more honestly. Mixing layers (e.g. a forecast model that also picks the base price) is what triggers the harder legal reviews.

100+ routes — partial pooling per layer

With 6,700 OD-pairs in the network and 141 fine-tuned, the cross-route statistical decision is different at each layer:

LayerPooling decisionWhyStatus
L1 Forecast Global trunk + per-family LoRA (partial pool at family). Foundation-model trunk handles cross-series transfer; LoRA captures family idiosyncrasies; works even on cold-start. Shipped (this portfolio).
L2 Elasticity Partial pool at OD level, nested in family priors. Low-volume routes have noisy per-OD elasticity; group priors shrink them toward family means. GPBoost or hierarchical Bayes. Recommended; OR team owns. Not shipped today.
L3 Base-price opt Independent per OD, with cross-elasticity coupling on substitutable ODs. Each OD has its own demand curve; coupling matters where shoppers substitute (DFW-LAX vs DFW-BUR vs DFW-LGB). Status quo at most airlines.
L4 Bot Per-OD bandit with shared prior over context features. Per-OD bandit explores cheaply; shared prior accelerates learning on cold-start ODs. Pricing Tech roadmap.

How wrong base prices fail in production

Three diagnostics I recommend the OR team monitor:

  1. Fare-ladder skew. Fraction of bookings that land at the floor or ceiling. If > 15% at either extreme, the band is mis-set; the optimizer is hitting the constraint, not the optimum.
  2. Reservation-price residuals. Conversion at price p minus expected conversion at price p from the elasticity model. Persistent positive residual means base too low; persistent negative means base too high.
  3. Analyst override rate per OD. RM analysts override base prices when they think the optimizer is wrong. A high override rate (> 8%) on a route family is a flag the base-price optimizer or its inputs are stale.

Interview Q&A — base price & the four-layer stack

Why is the base price more important when a bot runs the dynamic loop?
The bot acts within the base. A bad base survives the bot — bot lifts 20% of $150 instead of 20% of $220, you lose the same dollars across millions of seats. A good base + good bot > great bot + bad base. Bots optimise the deviation; the base decides what's being deviated from.
Where does the base price live in your portfolio today?
It doesn't. Today the portfolio owns the forecast layer (L1). Operations Research owns elasticity (L2); Pricing Strategy owns the base-price optimizer (L3); Pricing Tech owns the bot (L4). This page calls out the gap explicitly so interviewers don't assume the forecast is doing more than it does.
Could TimesFM produce a base price directly?
No. TimesFM forecasts realised fares under existing policy. The base price is a counterfactual policy choice (“what would happen if we set the anchor here?”). Treating a forecast coefficient as a policy lever is the most common mistake in airline RM and it's why we keep the layers separate.
Walk me through the four layers and what model class is right for each.
L1 forecast: foundation TS (TimesFM) for cross-route transfer + cold-start. L2 elasticity: GBM with random effects (GPBoost) or hierarchical Bayes for partial pooling; Double ML / DeepIV when you need causal identification. L3 base-price: MIP with offline Bayesian-opt for constraint settings. L4 bot: contextual bandits (Thompson sampling) with floor/ceiling guardrails.
How would you detect that the base price is wrong in production?
Three diagnostics. (1) Fare-ladder skew — bookings clumping at floor or ceiling. (2) Reservation-price residuals from the L2 elasticity model. (3) Analyst override rate per OD. Persistent override > 8% on a family is a flag.
What's the feedback-loop risk if elasticity is re-estimated from realised bookings?
Selection bias. Bookings reflect customers who accepted the offered price. Standard fixes: instrument with exogenous price variation (calendar-randomised A/B tests), or use Double ML / DeepIV with a credible instrument (fuel-price shock, FX shock). Without one of those, your estimated elasticity contracts toward the policy you ran.
Why not let one big RL policy subsume all four layers?
Four reasons. (1) Auditability — legal and revenue management need to explain strategic prices, not show an RL policy and shrug. (2) Antitrust — layer-specific reviews are easier than “one giant policy.” (3) Training-data sparsity at the strategic horizon — bandits learn fast; strategic anchors change weekly with limited samples. (4) Bot exploration cost — bandits explore into bad prices; OK at the per-request layer, not OK at the strategic anchor that customers see in advertising.
What changes when the competitor also runs a bot?
The single-agent base-price math is mis-specified — you're solving an equilibrium problem with a non-equilibrium tool. The right answer is multi-agent forecasting or inverse optimization (infer competitor's objective from their actions). For v1 we keep the “competitor fare as covariate” approach and flag it as a known mis-specification; the next research bet is a Stackelberg leader-follower model on the top-20 competitive ODs.
