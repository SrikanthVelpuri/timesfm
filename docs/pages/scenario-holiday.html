Scenario 1 — The Thanksgiving rush

The week before Thanksgiving is the highest-fare, highest-variance period of the domestic-US calendar. Holiday-specific demand pushes fares +$95 above baseline on domestic-hub routes. The model has to call the peak right or RMS leaves money on the table.

TL;DR. On the 6 peak days (Wed before through Sun after Thanksgiving), the model lifts median forecast +$95 on hub routes, +$140 on leisure-hub routes, and widens q90 to absorb peak-day variance. The C++ optimizer protects high-yield fare classes earlier, books less low-class inventory, and captures roughly +$11M of network revenue across the holiday week vs the prior ARIMAX-based forecast.

Trigger

It is October 22. The 30-day RMS forecast horizon now spans Nov 26 (Wed before) through Dec 1 (Sun after). The pricing team's federal-holiday calendar marked these days as holiday_flag = 2 (peak) months ago. The schedule is published, fuel hedges are booked, the school calendar is known.

The model has every input it needs to know the peak is coming, except the actual fare history of those days — which is in the future.

Model behavior

TimesFM does NOT “detect” the holiday on its own. It would, given enough history, learn the cyclic pattern that fares spike around day-of-year 330 ish. But the XReg head makes this explicit: the holiday_flag == 2 coefficient is +$87 (network-average), with family-specific multipliers.

A narrow PI would over-claim certainty.

Optimizer response

The C++ bid-price optimizer reads (q10, q50, q90) and constructs a fare-class inventory ladder. On Thanksgiving Wed the optimizer's logic looks like: 

# Reservation level for Y class (highest fare)
# = q90, because we're willing to leave seats empty if no one pays
bid_Y = $575

# Reservation level for B class (mid-tier)
# = q50, the expected fare
bid_B = $491

# Reservation level for Q class (deep discount)
# = max(q10, lowest_filed_fare)
bid_Q = $420   # high enough that AA closes most Q inventory

# Action:
#   - Open Y, B classes; protect aggressive inventory there.
#   - Close Q class on this date (already did 4 days ago).
#   - Continue to monitor; if competitor fares drop, re-evaluate.

Trade-offs

How wide should q90 be?

Wide q90 (aggressive)

  • Captures more revenue on truly-peak days when fares spike to $620.
  • Optimizer protects high-yield seats longer.
  • If actual is $620, you're happy you didn't close at $500.

Wide q90 (downside)

  • If actual fare is $480, you're holding empty seats at $570 with nobody paying.
  • Empty seats on peak days are 100% revenue loss; no downstream demand to absorb.
  • Competitor undercuts can leave AA pricing uncompetitive on the day-of.

The pinball loss training implicitly handles this trade-off — the model is rewarded for tight intervals when calibration permits. Empirically our q90 PI width on holidays (~$160) is about 2× non-holiday width — matched to the historical holiday variance.

Aggressive vs conservative interpretation by the optimizer

The optimizer can be configured to use q50 or q90 as the reservation level for Y class. AA chose q90 (aggressive) for the holiday week and q50 (conservative) for the surrounding days. The decision lives in the optimizer policy, not the model — keeping the model output policy-agnostic is a design rule.

What can go wrong, and how we'd know

  1. Holiday under-call. The XReg coefficient drifts (e.g., the COVID-era cohort got too much weight). Symptom: holiday-day MAPE > 9% on the val cohort. Mitigation: stratified sampler that over-weights Q4 days during fine-tune.
  2. Holiday over-call. A drift in the opposite direction; we forecast +$95 when the day was actually +$60 (e.g., a recession year). Symptom: empty seats, low load factor. Mitigation: monitor realized vs forecast specifically on holiday days.
  3. Competitor de-peaks. Delta decides not to match the AA holiday lift. The model's competitor-elasticity coefficient (~0.55 on hub routes) pulls our forecast down by ~$5–$10 the next morning. Optimizer re-prices the affected days via off-cycle re-score.

Counterfactual: what if we did NOT have the holiday covariate

Hypothetical — without the holiday_flag covariate, TimesFM would rely entirely on history-cycles. Three problems:

  • Insufficient cycles. 90 days of history covers maybe one Labor Day. Thanksgiving is ~9 months away from the context window. Model can't see it.
  • Date-of-year ambiguity. Thanksgiving moves ±6 days year-to-year. A pure date-of-year cycle would smear the peak across two weeks.
  • Drift. Without the flag, every change to the holiday calendar (e.g., AA shifts Easter day) requires retraining.

This is exactly why the foundation model alone (zero-shot, no covariates) is not enough. Holiday MAPE was 14.1% zero-shot → 8.7% with the XReg head. The +$95 coefficient is doing real work.

Real-world variants

  • Christmas / New Year. Same pattern, different magnitudes. flag=2 effect ~+$110 on the Dec 23–Dec 26 cohort.
  • Easter. flag=1 (federal holiday tier). +$26 on average. Less variance because Easter weekend is shorter.
  • Spring Break. No federal holiday flag, but school_break == 1 on leisure routes adds +$22.
  • Super Bowl city pair. Special-event covariate (NOT shipped in v1) would handle this; currently captured implicitly via competitor-fare spikes.

Interview Q&A

How does TimesFM know it's Thanksgiving on Nov 27 if the context only has 90 days of pre-Thanksgiving data?
It doesn't — not from history. The holiday_flag covariate explicitly tells it. The XReg head learned (during Stage A fine-tune) that holiday_flag == 2 means +$87 on the median; that coefficient applies to any day flagged as 2, regardless of whether the model has “seen” that calendar date in context. This is the value of the covariate-conditioning approach: known future structure feeds directly into the forecast.
What would you do if the holiday MAPE was 11% in the val cohort instead of 8.7%?
Three diagnostics. (1) Holiday samples in train cohort — how many? If <1% of training windows have flag=2, the coefficient is under-fit; over-weight via stratified sampling. (2) Heterogeneity across families — is the 11% concentrated on one family? If yes, that family needs a richer rank LoRA or more training windows. (3) Calibration vs accuracy — if MAPE is bad but coverage is good, it's about q50 not the distribution; usually a sign of a missing covariate or interaction term.
Why is the q90 wider on holidays? Is that a bug?
It's a feature, not a bug. The pinball-loss training learned that holiday variance is structurally higher (some Thanksgivings hit $620, others $440 depending on weekday-of-month, weather, and competitor moves). A narrow q90 would be over-confident; the wider PI is the model honestly admitting uncertainty. The optimizer uses the wider band to protect more high-yield inventory, which is the correct behavior.
Could you replace the holiday flag with day-of-year embeddings?
Tried it as an ablation. Day-of-year embedding alone gives 9.4% holiday MAPE (vs 8.7% with explicit flag). The embedding learns "around day 330 fares are high" but smears across the calendar week and doesn't handle moveable holidays (Easter, Thanksgiving). Could combine both — an embedding for general seasonality plus a flag for specific holidays — that gave 8.5% MAPE, marginal gain over flag-only and not worth the engineering. Stuck with flag.
If a stakeholder asks "why is the fare higher on Thanksgiving?", how do you answer?
Decompose the forecast: final = baseline + sum(covariate_contributions). On Nov 27 the contribution table shows holiday +$87, competitor +$15, DTD +$11, others zero or negligible. I can hand the stakeholder a single chart showing each component's dollar contribution. This is the audit trail compliance asked for — not just a single number, but a justifiable decomposition.
