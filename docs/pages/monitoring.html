Production monitoring & rollback

The model is in production for 26+ weeks. Real failures have happened. This page is the alerting design, the drift-detection methods, the rollback path, and the runbook I wrote for the on-call rotation.

TL;DR. Three drift types tracked: data drift (PSI on covariate distributions), concept drift (rolling MAPE), calibration drift (rolling 80% PI coverage). Six SLOs with PagerDuty alerts. One-command rollback per stage (XReg head OR LoRA OR both). Mean time to recover from real incidents in production: 22 minutes.

Three kinds of drift

1. Data drift — PSI (Population Stability Index)

For each numeric covariate, bin the recent distribution and compare to the training distribution:

PSI = Σbins ( precent − ptrain ) · ln( precent / ptrain )
  • PSI < 0.1: stable
  • 0.1 ≤ PSI < 0.25: moderate shift — watch
  • PSI ≥ 0.25: significant shift — investigate

For categorical covariates: chi-squared test on bin counts, with the same threshold bands.

2. Concept drift — rolling MAPE

Rolling 7-day MAPE on actuals as they materialize. Compare to the training-cohort MAPE. Page if the gap exceeds 1.5× the training MAPE's standard deviation.

Concept drift is the model itself becoming wrong — the relationship between covariates and target shifted. Different from data drift, which is just inputs changing. Possible triggers: AA introduces a new fare-class structure; competitor enters a new market; macro economic shift.

3. Calibration drift — coverage on actuals

The most operationally important. Rolling 7-day 80% PI coverage on the top-50 OD-pairs by revenue. If coverage drops below 75% on any family for 3 consecutive nights, PagerDuty pages me.

Why 3 consecutive nights and not 1? Single-night noise is high (~50 actuals on a small family); 3-night persistence filters out random fluctuation while still catching real drift fast.

One-command rollback

The rollback CLI was the single most important piece of MLOps in the project. It lets a half-asleep on-call engineer revert the service in under 30 seconds without needing to remember any AWS console paths. 

# Roll back the XReg head only (LoRA adapters keep current versions)
aa_pricing_cli rollback --stage head --to v20251104

# Roll back a specific LoRA family (everything else current)
aa_pricing_cli rollback --stage lora --family transcon --to v20251109

# Roll back everything to a specific run-id (the "oh no" button)
aa_pricing_cli rollback --to-run-id 20251110-001

# What this actually does:
#   1. Updates the model-registry pointer (atomic in DynamoDB).
#   2. Triggers an off-cycle re-score of the affected OD-pair set.
#   3. Logs the rollback event with cause/operator to BigQuery.
#   4. Posts a Slack message to #rms-pricing-oncall.

The runbook (excerpt)

Real text from the on-call runbook. Written so it works at 2 a.m. with no prior context. 

### Alert: COVERAGE_DRIFT_TOP50

You were paged because rolling 7-day 80% PI coverage on a top-50 OD-pair
family dropped below 75%.

### Quick Triage (90 seconds)

1. Open Datadog dashboard "AA Pricing — Health".
2. Look at row 2 — which family is red?
3. Look at row 4 — is any covariate PSI red on the same days?
4. If yes → upstream feature issue (skip to step 4).
   If no → model issue (skip to step 5).

### Mitigation Path A — Upstream feature

4a. Check the feature's Airflow DAG status:
    https://airflow.aa-rms.internal/dags/aa_pricing_features
4b. If the DAG failed: page the data team via #data-oncall
4c. Set the feature to "stale, use last known":
    aa_pricing_cli feature mark-stale --feature competitor_fare
4d. Re-score affected OD-pairs:
    aa_pricing_cli rescore --family 
4e. Watch coverage recover over next 24 h.

### Mitigation Path B — Model issue

5a. Roll back the family's LoRA adapter to last week's version:
    aa_pricing_cli rollback --stage lora --family  \
        --to $(aa_pricing_cli list-versions --stage lora --family  --limit 2 | tail -1)
5b. Re-score the family.
5c. Open ticket in JIRA project AAPM, severity P2, attach Datadog snapshot.
5d. Investigate next business day — do NOT debug at 2 a.m.

### Escalation

If neither path resolves within 30 minutes, page Sarah at +1-XXX-XXX-XXXX.

The auto-rollback safety net

Beyond manual rollback, we have one automated rollback trigger: if a freshly-deployed model produces >3% NaN forecasts in the first 100 batches, the deploy is auto-reverted and the on-call is paged.

This caught a real incident: a LoRA adapter trained on a route family with extreme fare variance produced NaN forecasts when the temperature scaling clipped the q90 to +∞. The auto-rollback caught it in 4 batches; nobody was paged at 2 a.m.; the investigation happened the next morning.

What I do NOT alert on (intentionally)

  • Single-night MAPE excursions. Holiday days routinely produce 12% MAPE; alerting on this would page me 8 nights a quarter for nothing.
  • Per-OD-pair coverage. Statistically meaningless on 30 daily samples; only the family-level rollup is reliable.
  • PSI fluctuations on day-of-week and holiday flag. These are designed to drift — the holiday flag is non-zero only on holidays. Alerting on PSI here is noise.
  • Cost per individual run. Spot pricing fluctuates; alert only on rolling 7-day spend.

Real incidents

  1. Week 19 — ATPCO competitor feed lag. 18-hour stale data caused coverage on Caribbean leisure to drop to 71%. Detected 3 nights in. Mitigated via feature-stale flag in 22 minutes.
  2. Week 28 — LoRA family overfit on a hot route. JFK-LAS family produced unusually tight q90s after a re-train; coverage dropped to 73%. Rolled back to previous adapter, retrained with stratified sampling next morning.
  3. Week 35 — cold weather event. Unusual ice storm at DFW — weather covariate flag was correctly set, but the model under-predicted the magnitude of irrops fare suppression. Caught the next morning, no alert (within tolerance), filed for next quarter's training data refresh.

What I would build next if I had time

  • Out-of-distribution detector on the input embeddings (Mahalanobis distance from training distribution, per-batch). Would catch black-swan inputs faster than per-covariate PSI.
  • Live A/B framework: stand up a champion-challenger split where the challenger is a candidate Stage-A retrain. Currently we only do shadow mode.
  • Auto-disable LoRA family on coverage breach instead of paging me. Trade-off: fewer pages, but harder to diagnose retrospectively because the rollback erases the evidence.

Interview Q&A

How do you distinguish data drift from concept drift in production?
Data drift = inputs changed (PSI on covariates). Concept drift = relationship between inputs and target changed (rolling MAPE on actuals goes up even when PSI is stable). Diagnostically: if both drift, it's usually data drift; if MAPE drifts but PSI stays low, it's real concept drift and you need a retrain. ~80% of "drift" alerts in our system are data drift caused by upstream feed issues, not actual concept drift.
Why PSI >= 0.25 as the threshold?
Standard convention from credit-risk modelling. PSI > 0.25 corresponds to roughly KL divergence > 0.1, which is the threshold above which a model trained on the old distribution starts to lose calibration on the new one. Empirically tested on AA: thresholds < 0.25 produce too many false-positive alerts; > 0.25 misses real shifts. The number is convention; the principle is "alert at the level that empirically catches real issues without flooding on-call."
Walk me through what you do in the first 5 minutes of a 2 a.m. coverage drift page.
First 30 seconds: open the Datadog dashboard, find which family is red. 30s–90s: check covariate PSI on the same days — if any covariate is red, it's upstream. 90s–3 min: if upstream, mark feature stale and re-score the family. 3–5 min: if not upstream, roll back the family's LoRA. Total intervention <5 min. Investigation happens next morning — the runbook says explicitly “do NOT debug at 2 a.m.”
How do you decide on alert thresholds without flooding on-call?
Run the proposed alert against 90 days of historical metrics. Count how many times it would have fired. If it's more than ~1 per month for a single SLO, the threshold is too tight. If it's less than ~1 per quarter, it's too loose — you won't catch real issues. Goal: ~3–5 real pages per quarter total across the service. We hit that bar.
Why do you separate “warn at” from “page at”?
Warns go to a Slack channel; pages go to a phone. Warns let me see something is sliding before it's an incident, so I can investigate next business day. Pages are reserved for “customer impact in <1 hour if I do nothing.” The page threshold is roughly 1.5× the warn threshold — matched to how fast the metric typically moves once it starts drifting.
How do you test a rollback works before you need it?
Synthetic on-call drill once per quarter. The team lead picks a Saturday and "deploys" a known-bad model to canary; I get paged and have to resolve. We measure mean time to recover. First drill in week 15: 6 minutes 12 seconds. Second drill in week 32: 4 minutes 51 seconds. The drill catches runbook bugs before real incidents do.
If you were starting over, what monitoring would you build first, and why?
Coverage on actuals, on a per-route-family rolling basis. It's the single most informative signal for “is the model right or wrong” in a probabilistic forecasting context. Latency and cost are also important but they fail safely — you find out fast because the dashboard goes red. Coverage can drift silently for weeks if you're not measuring it. Build it on day 1.
