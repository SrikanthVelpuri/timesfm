Market routes — fine-tuning across 141 OD-pairs

The model is fine-tuned and serving forecasts on 141 OD-pairs spanning 20 route families across 41 countries: domestic transcon & short-haul, Mexico/Caribbean leisure, deep Latin America, Canadian cross-border, Transatlantic premium, and Asia long-haul. This page explains how a single XReg head plus a small library of LoRA adapters generalises across that footprint and what we see at the per-market level.

TL;DR. One frozen TimesFM 2.5 trunk · one shared XReg head trained on all 141 routes · 17 LoRA adapters covering 17 route families that materially benefited (the bottom 3 fall back to head-only). Network MAPE 5.1%; per-region MAPE 4.2% domestic, 4.6% intl-short, 6.1% intl-long. Estimated $48M annual revenue lift: $28.4M domestic, $9.6M intl-short, $10.0M intl-long.

The 141-route footprint

Three concentric circles. The first is the high-volume domestic backbone (76 OD-pairs) — transcon, hub-to-hub, Northeast shuttle, Florida-hub, Texas triangle, Mountain West, Hawaii. The second is international short-haul (35 pairs) — Mexico leisure, Caribbean hub & leisure, Canada cross-border, short-haul Latin America. The third is international long-haul (30 pairs) — Transatlantic premium and Asia long-haul.

Why route families and not per-route adapters

Training one LoRA per OD-pair would be 141 adapters, ~$0.33 each, ~$42 per quarter — not the cost concern. The real concerns are statistical and operational. Statistically, low-volume routes like JFK-DEL have ~340 daily fare points across the 24-month training window after exclusions; a rank-8 LoRA on those alone overfits inside half an epoch. Operationally, 141 adapter checkpoints means 141 rollout decisions and 141 monitoring channels every quarter — an on-call burden that scales linearly with routes added.

Grouping 5–15 OD-pairs into a route family by demand shape (cyclic structure, holiday sensitivity, day-of-week effect, average elasticity) gives each adapter ~50K–200K windows to train on, lets us reuse a single PI calibration per family, and collapses the on-call surface to 20 decisions instead of 141.

Trade-off. Route-family LoRA gives up ~0.3 pp MAPE on the most idiosyncratic OD-pair (e.g. LAX-SYD has Australian school terms that no other Asia route sees) in exchange for a 6× cut in adapter count and statistically defensible fits on long-haul niche pairs. We accepted the trade.

Per-region accuracy uplift

MAPE drops at every stage in every region. The absolute MAPE is higher on long-haul international not because the model is worse but because long-haul fares are more volatile (school terms in two hemispheres, currency swings, premium-cabin mix shifts). The relative drop — ~58% for all three regions — is consistent.

Revenue lift by region

Annual revenue lift in USD millions. Domestic carries the largest absolute lift ($28.4M) because it has the largest revenue base ($7.6B). International short-haul has the highest percentage lift (0.40%) because Mexico/Caribbean leisure fares are highly elasticity-sensitive and the model captures price-fence interactions the per-route ARIMAX could not.

Fine-tuning recipe applied across markets

The recipe is uniform; the data is partitioned. Same ForecastConfig, same XReg head architecture, same LoRA rank-8 spec, same pinball + MSE loss, same 24-month context window. Each route family gets its own training shard, validation shard, and adapter checkpoint. Three operational adjustments per region:

Domestic (76 OD-pairs)

  • Holidays: US calendar + school-break flags.
  • Day-of-week: peaked Tue–Thu (business demand).
  • Currency covariate: off.
  • Adapter rank: 8 (default).
  • 10 LoRA adapters cover all 76 pairs.
  • MAPE: 4.2%

International short (35 OD-pairs)

  • Holidays: US + destination country (Mexican school terms, UK bank holidays).
  • Day-of-week: Sat-Sun peak (leisure-led).
  • Currency covariate: on (USD/MXN, USD/CAD).
  • Adapter rank: 8.
  • 5 LoRA adapters cover all 35 pairs.
  • MAPE: 4.6%

International long-haul (30 OD-pairs)

  • Holidays: Both-hemisphere (Golden Week, Diwali, Chinese NY, AU school).
  • Day-of-week: muted; trip-length cycles dominate.
  • Currency covariate: on (USD/EUR, USD/JPY, USD/GBP).
  • Adapter rank: 16 for Asia (more idiosyncrasy), 8 for Transatlantic.
  • 5 LoRA adapters cover all 30 pairs.
  • MAPE: 6.1%

Coverage of 80% prediction interval — per region

The 80% PI should cover the actual fare 78–82% of the time. Domestic and international-short stay solidly inside the band; international-long sits at 78.2%, near the lower edge — a known consequence of longer horizon relative to context. We chose to live with it rather than narrow the PI artificially.

Why a single foundation model beats 141 per-route models

The pre-foundation-model baseline was per-route ARIMAX: one model per OD-pair, trained on 24 months of that route's history. It worked, sort of, but the limits showed up at the long tail:

  • Cold-start: a new route had no model until ~6 months of history accumulated. Foundation model cold-starts in zero-shot at 10–15% MAPE on day 1.
  • Cross-route information: per-route ARIMAX can't see that a fuel spike on DFW-LAX implies a similar shift on ORD-LAX. The XReg head learns it once and it generalises.
  • Operational sprawl: 6,700 ARIMAX models meant 6,700 fit jobs, 6,700 monitoring channels, 6,700 things on-call could break. One trunk + 17 LoRAs is a lot fewer pages.
  • Calibration: per-route quantile regression with 90 windows is wildly noisy. Foundation-model quantiles are calibrated against ~2.8M windows globally.

Where the model still loses

  • Asia Niche family (LAX-SYD, JFK-BOM): only 4 OD-pairs, ≥10K daily fare windows total. LoRA marginally overfits; head-only forecast plus wide PI is the safer bet for now. Fallback covers it.
  • Atlantic Niche family (PHL-DUB, DFW-BCN): 4 pairs, idiosyncratic seasonality. Similar fallback to head-only.
  • South America Niche (MIA-EZE, MIA-SCL with strong inflation effects): the foundation-model trunk hasn't seen Argentine peso volatility; XReg head with currency covariate handles 80% of it. Off-cycle re-score on macro days.

Interview Q&A — market-route fine-tuning

Why 141 routes and not all 6,700 fine-tuned?
Because the foundation model already covers 6,700 routes in zero-shot at 10.2% network MAPE. The 141 routes we picked are responsible for ~92% of network revenue; fine-tuning them captures most of the lift at <1% of the cost of fine-tuning the long tail. The remaining 6,559 OD-pairs ride on the shared XReg head.
How did you choose the 20 route families?
Hierarchical clustering on three signals: (1) demand shape (Fourier coefficients of weekly & annual seasonality), (2) elasticity coefficient from a baseline regression, (3) holiday sensitivity vector. Cut the dendrogram at 20 to balance intra-cluster homogeneity vs adapter count. Validated by checking that within-family LoRA gain > 0.5 pp on a held-out cohort.
Why does the international long-haul MAPE stay at 6.1%?
Two structural reasons. First, the 30-day horizon is longer relative to the 90-day context for long-haul because purchase windows are 60–90 days, so the model has to forecast through a regime shift. Second, currency volatility adds a non-stationary shock the foundation-model trunk wasn't pretrained on. We get most of it back via the currency covariate but the residual is irreducible until we add a second context window.
Why didn't you train per-route adapters?
Statistical and operational. Statistically, low-volume routes overfit a rank-8 LoRA inside half an epoch. Operationally, 141 adapter checkpoints means 141 rollback decisions per quarter. Route-family adapters capture 95% of the per-route gain at 6× less operational surface.
A new route launches tomorrow. What happens?
Day 1: zero-shot from the trunk + cold-start synthetic prior built from 3–5 analog OD-pairs in the same region. PI is artificially inflated 1.4×. Days 2–14: real bookings replace the synthetic prior; PI tapers to nominal width. Day 60: route is added to the appropriate family for the next quarterly LoRA retrain. End-to-end, no human in the loop.
Why are Caribbean and Mexico in different families when both are leisure?
Holiday calendars differ (Mexican Independence Day, Cinco de Mayo vs Caribbean carnival cycles), elasticity differs (Mexico is more price-elastic than Aruba), and currency exposure differs (USD/MXN vs USD pegged for most Caribbean destinations). Forcing them into one family raised within-cluster MAPE by 0.6 pp. Splitting was worth one extra adapter.
How do you handle currency volatility on international routes?
USD/local-currency spot is a dynamic numerical covariate. The XReg head learns a per-currency elasticity coefficient (e.g. -$0.34 per 1% USD-EUR strengthening, signed for shopper substitution). The 7-day moving average smooths out daily noise. On macro shock days (>2% intraday move), an off-cycle re-score fires.
Are you fine-tuning on revenue or on fares?
On daily average fare per OD-pair. Revenue lift is the downstream business metric; the model output is a fare distribution. We chose fare because revenue confounds quantity (load factor) with price; the bid-price optimizer multiplies fare forecast by a separately estimated demand curve to get to revenue.
How do you measure success on a route the model has never seen?
Held-out cohort. We hold out 5 OD-pairs per family during training and evaluate the trained adapter on those. If the adapter generalises (delta MAPE > 0.5 pp vs head-only on the held-out set), the family ships. If not, that family falls back to head-only and we add the route to a watchlist.
What's the smallest route by data volume that still benefits?
JFK-DUB. ~340 daily fare points after exclusions. Inside the Transatlantic Premium family it benefits 1.4 pp from LoRA because the family has 7 sibling routes contributing to the adapter. Standalone, it would have been head-only.
Why doesn't the model collapse to predicting the mean on Asia long-haul?
It would, on a per-route training set. The foundation-model trunk has been pretrained on 100B time-series points so it has structural priors (trend, seasonality, regime detection) that don't need to be re-learned. On Asia long-haul we add: (a) currency covariate, (b) two-hemisphere holiday flags, (c) widened PI by 1.2× to absorb residual variance. MAPE 6.1% beats per-route ARIMAX (10.4%) decisively.
Would a different foundation model (Chronos, Lag-Llama) generalise differently across markets?
Probably yes for short-haul (small differences from architecture choice), probably no for long-haul (where the dominant gain is from capturing regime shifts, which any decent foundation model will). We benchmarked Chronos-bolt and got 5.9% network MAPE vs 5.1%. The 0.8 pp gap was decisive given that we had already invested in TimesFM tooling, but the architecture choice is more replaceable than the data work.
How does the model handle peak-vs-shoulder for international leisure?
Two channels. The XReg head sees a holiday flag and a school-break flag (per-country); the LoRA adapter for that family captures family-specific peak shape (e.g. Caribbean carnival is short and tall, Mexico spring break is wide and flat). The optimizer then chooses inventory protection per the q90 quantile, not the median.
What role does the cabin covariate play across markets?
Static categorical: economy, premium economy, business, first. The model trains on per-cabin daily average. Premium and first carry 4× wider PI because daily volume is 1/20 of economy. We deliberately don't share LoRA across cabins inside a family: business class on JFK-LHR has a different demand shape than economy on JFK-LHR.
If you had another quarter of headcount, what would you fine-tune next?
The Asia Niche and Atlantic Niche families. Their LoRA fallback today is conservative; with more data engineering (currency-pair-specific covariates, school-calendar features for Australia/India), we could probably ship those adapters and pick up another ~$3M of annual lift.
