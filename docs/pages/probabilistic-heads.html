Probabilistic output heads — how a model emits an uncertainty

A point forecast is one number. A probabilistic forecast is a distribution — or a quantile sweep, or a sample path, or a conformal interval. The choice of output head determines what kind of uncertainty you can express and how much you can trust it. evaluation.html covered the metrics (pinball, CRPS, ECE, coverage); this page covers the architectures that produce the distributions those metrics measure.

TL;DR. Five output-head families. (1) Quantile head — predict q10..q90 directly with pinball loss; what TimesFM 2.5 ships. (2) Parametric distributional — predict mean+variance of Gaussian / Student-T / NegBin (DeepAR, Lag-Llama). (3) Discrete-token head — bin into 4096 buckets and sample as if it were language (Chronos-T5). (4) Mixture distribution — weighted combination (Moirai). (5) Conformal prediction — post-hoc wrapper that gives finite-sample coverage guarantees regardless of head choice. We ship continuous quantile head (TimesFM 2.5 native) + conformal-prediction wrapper on top for hard coverage guarantees.

Why output-head choice matters

Three things depend entirely on the head you choose. First, the loss function you can train with — pinball loss for quantiles, NLL for parametric, cross-entropy for tokens. Second, the kind of question you can answer at inference — P(y > threshold) is easy with parametric, hard with quantile. Third, the calibration story — conformal prediction gives you finite-sample guarantees; everything else is asymptotic.

1 · Quantile head — predict q10..q90 directly

Train the model to emit K quantile estimates per future timepoint. Loss is the pinball (quantile regression) loss, summed over quantiles: 

Pinball loss for quantile q in (0, 1):
  L_q(y, y_hat) = max(q * (y - y_hat), (q - 1) * (y - y_hat))

Total loss:
  L = sum over q in {0.1, 0.2, ..., 0.9} of L_q(y, y_hat_q)

Two flavours: discrete (one head per quantile, K independent outputs) and continuous (one head that parameterises a monotone CDF, sampled at any q at inference). v1 of TimesFM was discrete-K; v2.5 is continuous — eliminates the “non-monotone quantile” bug where q60 < q50 by construction.

Continuous monotone parameterisation

The trick: parameterise the CDF as a cumulative-softplus over learned bin edges. Output a vector e[0..B] of bin edges and monotone increments d[1..B] = softplus(raw[1..B]). The CDF inverse is then constructed by linear interpolation between cumulative sums. By construction q1 < q2 implies forecast(q1) < forecast(q2). No post-hoc fixup needed. 

Output head:
  raw     = MLP(hidden) -> (128, B)         # 128 future timepoints, B bin edges
  delta   = softplus(raw)                   # positive increments
  cdf_inv = e0 + cumsum(delta)              # monotone in q

For any quantile q in (0, 1):
  forecast_q = linear_interp(cdf_inv, q * (B-1))
  • Pros: trains with pinball loss; calibration is direct; head architecture is small (one MLP); supports any quantile at inference.
  • Cons: pinball loss is heuristic if you actually want a likelihood; coverage is asymptotic, not finite-sample (use conformal on top).
  • When to pick: TS forecasting, audit-friendly defaults. Our pick.

2 · Parametric distributional head

Predict the parameters of a known distribution per future timepoint. Train with negative log-likelihood (NLL). Inference: sample from the predicted distribution, or compute exact quantiles in closed form.

Gaussian head (DeepAR, classical)

head outputs (mu, sigma) per timepoint:
  mu    = linear(hidden)
  sigma = softplus(linear(hidden))   # positivity
NLL loss:
  L = 0.5 * log(2*pi*sigma^2) + 0.5 * (y - mu)^2 / sigma^2
At inference:
  q_alpha = mu + sigma * z_alpha     # closed-form quantiles
  P(y > t) = 1 - Phi((t - mu) / sigma)

Cheap, well-calibrated when the residual is Gaussian. Fails on heavy tails (jumps blow up sigma; model under-predicts extremes) and on bounded domains (Gaussian can predict negative fares).

Student-T head (Lag-Llama, robust forecasting)

head outputs (mu, sigma, df):
  df  = softplus(linear(hidden)) + 2     # df > 2 for finite variance
NLL closed-form for Student-T.

Heavy-tailed distribution; robust to outliers. Trades calibration for tail coverage — useful for fraud / anomaly forecasting, overkill for AA fare data which is well-Gaussian past normalization.

Negative Binomial head (count data)

head outputs (mu, alpha):                # alpha is overdispersion
NLL closed-form for NegBin.

Right answer for count data: bookings per minute, requests per second, failures per day. Variance > mean is allowed; Poisson breaks down the moment overdispersion appears, NegBin handles it.

Mixture distributional head (Moirai, robust foundation)

head outputs k components and weights:
  for i in 1..k:
      (mu_i, sigma_i, family_i) = MLP(hidden)
  weights w = softmax(linear(hidden))
log-likelihood:
  L = -log(sum_i w_i * pdf(family_i; mu_i, sigma_i)(y))

Mixture of Gaussian + Student-T + lognormal handles heterogeneous series (some bounded, some heavy-tailed) without pre-classification. Moirai uses this for its “any-distribution” foundation. Cost: more parameters, slower convergence, harder to debug.

  • Pros (parametric): closed-form quantiles, P(y > t), and CRPS; fewer parameters than quantile head; natural for Bayesian aggregation.
  • Cons (parametric): family choice is a strong prior; Gaussian fails on heavy tails; mixtures are unstable to train.
  • When to pick: when domain knowledge supplies the right family; count data needs NegBin; bounded data needs truncated.

3 · Discrete-token head (Chronos-T5)

Quantile-bin the series into B = 4,096 buckets (uniform on Z-scored values). Each bucket is a vocabulary token. The model is a vanilla T5 LM emitting one token per future timepoint. Probabilistic forecast: sample S = 20 token sequences from the decoder, denormalize, take empirical quantiles. 

# Tokenization
mu, sigma = mean_std(context)
z         = (y - mu) / sigma
buckets   = digitize(z, edges_4096)         # bucket id in [0, 4095]

# Training
loss = cross_entropy(logits, buckets)       # standard LM training

# Inference
S = 20
samples = []
for s in 1..S:
    tokens = decoder.sample(decoded_so_far) # autoregressive
    samples.append(unbin(tokens) * sigma + mu)
q_alpha = empirical_quantile(samples, alpha)
  • Pros: zero distributional assumption; every NLP technique transfers (sampling, beam search, in-context learning); strong on heavy-tailed series because uniform buckets allocate tail capacity.
  • Cons: bucket precision degrades at the extremes; autoregressive sampling is 20× slower; quantization noise floor.
  • When to pick: heavy-tailed series, language-model-like training pipeline preferred, latency budget allows sampling.

4 · Sample-based heads (DDPM-style, normalizing flows)

Two adjacent ideas. Diffusion-based forecasting (TimeGrad, ScoreGrad) trains a denoising network to reverse a noising process; samples are produced by 50–1000 reverse steps. Normalizing flows (RealNVP, MAF for TS) parameterise an invertible mapping from a simple base distribution to the data distribution; exact NLL and exact sampling.

  • Pros: most flexible distributional output; can capture multimodality; exact likelihoods (flows).
  • Cons: training is unstable; inference is slow (diffusion needs many reverse steps); production tooling immature.
  • When to pick: research; multimodal demand (e.g. one mode for normal day, one for holiday). Not in production at AA.

5 · Conformal prediction — post-hoc coverage guarantees

The only output mechanism with finite-sample coverage guarantees: under exchangeability, an α-conformal prediction interval contains the true value with probability ≥ 1 − α for any black-box model. Doesn't care if your model was a quantile head, parametric, sample-based, or a coin flip.

The basic recipe (split conformal)

# Calibration set:
res_i = |y_i - y_hat_i|       for i in calibration set
qhat = ceil((n+1) * (1-alpha)) / n -th quantile of {res_i}
# At inference:
PI = [y_hat - qhat, y_hat + qhat]
# Coverage guarantee: P(y in PI) >= 1 - alpha for any new exchangeable y.

Conformalized Quantile Regression (CQR, Romano et al. 2019)

# Use the model's own q_low, q_high as a starting point.
res_i = max(q_low_i - y_i, y_i - q_high_i)   # signed conformity score
qhat  = (1-alpha)-quantile of {res_i}
PI    = [q_low - qhat, q_high + qhat]

CQR is what we run on top of TimesFM's quantile head: coverage is empirical instead of asymptotic, and the band width adapts to local model confidence.

Weighted conformal (for non-exchangeable TS)

Pure conformal assumes the calibration set and test point are exchangeable. For TS that's violated by drift. Weighted conformal (Tibshirani et al.) re-weights the calibration set by importance weights to compensate. Or: use the most recent N calibration points to mitigate drift effects (sliding-window conformal).

  • Pros: distribution-free; finite-sample guarantees; trivially layered on top of any head.
  • Cons: requires a held-out calibration set; assumes exchangeability (which TS violates — weighted conformal is a partial fix); intervals are symmetric unless you use CQR.
  • When to pick: any time you need a hard coverage SLO. We use it.

What we ship at AA — the full output stack

(1) TimesFM 2.5 native head:
       continuous-quantile, monotone-by-construction
       output: q10, q20, ..., q90 for each future timepoint

(2) Conformalized Quantile Regression on top:
       per-route calibration set: last 90 days of holdout residuals
       qhat_route = (1-alpha)-quantile of conformity scores
       PI_route = [q10 - qhat_route, q90 + qhat_route]

(3) Coverage SLO monitoring:
       target: 80% PI covers actual 80 +/- 2% of the time
       window: rolling 30-day actuals
       breach: PSI alarm + paged ML-on-call

(4) Reporting outputs:
       q50  -> revenue-management point forecast
       q10  -> demand-floor for capacity planning
       q90  -> demand-ceiling for spoilage avoidance
       PI   -> conformal interval to RM dashboard

Why this stack and not a parametric Gaussian head: AA fare data is heavy-tailed enough at the extremes (last-minute price spikes, weather-driven crashes) that Gaussian under-covers q90 by 5–7 percentage points. We measured that in the bake-off. Pinball loss with conformal fixup gives us reliable coverage without committing to a distribution family.

Calibration tests we run

  • Coverage at 80% PI — expected 80 ± 2% on rolling 30-day window. Fail = PSI alarm.
  • Pinball at q10 and q90 — tail loss; spikes when extreme bookings shift.
  • ECE (Expected Calibration Error) — bucket forecasts by predicted quantile, measure empirical coverage. Reliability diagram should be near the diagonal.
  • PIT (Probability Integral Transform) histogram — CDF(actual) under the predicted distribution should be uniform on [0, 1]. A peaked PIT means under-coverage; a U-shaped PIT means over-coverage.
  • CRPS — Continuous Ranked Probability Score, the proper scoring rule for the full distribution. We track it as a single-number summary.

See evaluation.html for how each of these is computed in the eval harness.

Interview Q&A — probabilistic output heads

Why pinball loss instead of MLE?
Pinball loss directly targets quantiles without committing to a distribution family. MLE under e.g. Gaussian is more efficient if the data really is Gaussian, but on heavy-tailed AA fares, Gaussian NLL under-covers q90 by 5–7 percentage points. Pinball is the safer default; we use conformal prediction on top for hard coverage guarantees.
Walk me through the continuous-quantile head.
Output a vector of monotone-by-construction CDF values. The trick: parameterise via cumulative softplus over learned bin edges — output is e0 + cumsum(softplus(raw)). By construction the result is non-decreasing in q. At inference, sample any quantile by linear interpolation. Solves the v1/v2 bug where ~0.4% of forecasts had q60 < q50.
When does Gaussian NLL fail badly?
Three scenarios. (1) Heavy-tailed data — the variance estimate is dominated by extremes, q90 is biased low. (2) Bounded data — Gaussian predicts negative values; we'd need post-hoc clamp or a log-link. (3) Multi-modal data — Gaussian is unimodal, single mean and variance can't express two demand regimes. NegBin or mixture solves cases (1) and (3); truncated Gaussian solves (2).
What does conformal prediction buy you over a calibrated quantile head?
Finite-sample coverage guarantee. A quantile head's “80% PI” is a best-effort prediction; conformal calibrates it post-hoc with an exchangeability-based margin. Result: P(y in PI) ≥ 1 - alpha for any new exchangeable y, regardless of model error. Cheap to compute (one quantile over the calibration set), and the only output method with hard coverage promises.
Is conformal prediction valid for time series?
Pure conformal assumes exchangeability, which TS violates under drift. Two practical fixes. (1) Sliding-window conformal: use the last N residuals as calibration; coverage is approximately valid as long as drift is slow relative to N. (2) Weighted conformal (Tibshirani et al. 2019): re-weight calibration residuals by importance weights derived from a drift estimator. We use sliding-window in production with N = 90 days; weighted conformal is on the watch list for high-drift routes.
How does Chronos's discrete-token head compare to a quantile head?
Chronos quantile-bins values into 4,096 vocabulary tokens and trains a vanilla LM with cross-entropy. Probabilistic forecast comes from sampling 20 token paths and taking empirical quantiles. Pros: zero distribution assumption, every NLP trick (sampling, beam search) transfers. Cons: 20× inference cost, token quantization noise floor (precision ~0.001 sigma at the edges). For high-throughput batch forecasting, the cost is the deal-breaker.
What's CRPS and why do you track it alongside MAPE?
CRPS (Continuous Ranked Probability Score) is the integral of squared difference between the predicted CDF and the empirical CDF of the actual; it generalises MAE to distributions and is a proper scoring rule. MAPE measures only the q50 forecast; CRPS measures the whole distribution. A model that's 5% MAPE with a tight (under-confident) PI is worse than 5% MAPE with calibrated PI — CRPS catches that.
Walk me through the PIT histogram check.
For each forecast, compute the predicted CDF at the actual value — PIT(y) = F_predicted(y). If the model is well-calibrated, PIT values are uniform on [0, 1]. Plot the histogram of PIT values across the test set. A peaked-in-the-middle histogram means under-coverage (model overestimates uncertainty). A U-shape means over-coverage (model is overconfident). Cheap, visual, and catches calibration bugs that ECE alone misses.
When would you use a mixture-distribution head over a quantile head?
When the predictive distribution is genuinely multimodal — e.g. demand has a “normal day” mode and a “holiday” mode and you can't condition on a holiday flag because you don't know the holiday is coming. A mixture of Gaussians can express both modes; a quantile head will smear them together. For AA fares, daily demand is unimodal once you condition on day-of-week and holiday, so we don't need it.
Why didn't you ship a diffusion-based forecaster?
Two reasons. (1) Inference cost — 50–1000 reverse steps per sample × 20 samples is 1,000–20,000× the cost of one TimesFM forward pass. (2) Production maturity — the tooling is research-grade; we don't have an audit story. The accuracy gain over a well-calibrated quantile head is small for unimodal demand. We'd revisit if we forecast multimodal event-driven demand.
If you had to forecast minute-level booking volume, what head?
Negative Binomial. Booking volume is count data with overdispersion (peaks during fare drops, zeros at night). Poisson would underestimate variance; Gaussian would predict negative counts. NegBin's (mu, alpha) parameterisation handles overdispersion and stays non-negative. Train with NegBin NLL.
