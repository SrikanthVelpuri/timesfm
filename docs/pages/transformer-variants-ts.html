Transformer variants for TS — what each variant solves

Before foundation models, the field spent four years inventing transformer variants specialised for time series. The variants are still useful: they are the right pick when you have one panel, abundant history, and you want a small model fit per dataset. This page is the architectural deep-dive of the seven variants you will actually be asked about: Vanilla Transformer, Informer, Autoformer, FEDformer, PatchTST, Crossformer, iTransformer.

TL;DR. Three structural problems that vanilla transformers had on TS: (1) quadratic attention, (2) no signal decomposition, (3) per-timestep token wastes capacity on noisy multivariate channels. Each variant fixes one or more. Informer tackles attention complexity (ProbSparse). Autoformer tackles decomposition (trend / seasonal + autocorrelation attention). FEDformer moves attention into the frequency domain. PatchTST tokenises by patches and forecasts each variate independently. Crossformer attends twice: cross-time then cross-variate. iTransformer inverts the token axis — each variate is a token. For our 100+ cold-start panel, foundation models still win; for 1–10 well-known panels with deep history, PatchTST or iTransformer wins.

The three structural problems vanilla transformers had on TS

  1. O(L2) attention. Long-context TS (3+ years of daily data is L = 1095) blows past quadratic attention.
  2. No native decomposition. TS naturally decomposes into trend + seasonal + residual; vanilla attention has no inductive bias for this.
  3. One-token-per-timestep is wasteful. A token represents one timestep across all channels; capacity goes to noise more than signal, and channel correlations get baked into a single embedding.

1 · Vanilla Transformer (the baseline)

Self-attention over per-timestep tokens. Add learned absolute or sinusoidal positional encoding. Output is decoded autoregressively or with a parallel decoder. Almost nobody uses this on TS in 2026, because every variant below beats it on cost and accuracy for real benchmarks — but it's the reference architecture against which the variants are measured. 

x[1..L]      raw multivariate series (L timesteps, D channels)
   |
   v   per-timestep linear embed
tokens[1..L, d_model]    (one token per timestep)
   |
   v   transformer encoder x N (full L*L attention)
hidden[1..L, d_model]
   |
   v   linear head
y_hat[L+1..L+H, D]
  • Strengths: simple, well-understood, transfers from NLP literature.
  • Weaknesses: O(L2) attention; noise saturates per-timestep tokens; no decomposition.
  • When to pick: never, in 2026, for production TS. Useful as a research baseline.

2 · Informer (Zhou et al. 2021)

The first transformer variant that actually shipped on long-horizon TS benchmarks. Introduced ProbSparse self-attention: instead of computing all L2 attention scores, sample a small subset of queries based on their KL-divergence-from-uniform — queries that are “less informative” (closer to uniform) get dropped from the attention computation. Net cost is O(L log L).

Also introduced generative-style decoder: instead of autoregressive decoding, give the decoder a placeholder zero-padded target and let it produce the whole horizon in one pass. 

Encoder: stack of (ProbSparse-attention + distilling conv layer) blocks.
         Distilling conv halves the sequence length each layer.
Decoder: one masked ProbSparse attention + cross-attention.
         Output the full horizon in one shot.
Complexity: O(L log L) attention.
  • Strengths: sub-quadratic attention; one-shot decoder is fast.
  • Weaknesses: KL-based query sampling is a heuristic; loses signal on uniformly important contexts; later papers (PatchTST) showed simpler patching beats ProbSparse.
  • When to pick: extremely long horizons (H > 720), one panel, low compute budget. Largely superseded.

3 · Autoformer (Wu et al. 2021)

Two architectural ideas. First, series decomposition as an inductive bias: a moving-average filter splits each input into trend + seasonal components inside the encoder/decoder stack at every layer. The model learns each component separately and recombines at the end.

Second, autocorrelation attention replaces dot-product attention. Compute the autocorrelation function of the series in the frequency domain (FFT), find the top-k lags by autocorrelation, and aggregate via these lags. Captures period-level dependencies directly. 

For each layer:
  trend_t, seasonal_t = decompose(input)         # moving-average split
  trend_t  -> passthrough trend stack
  seasonal_t -> auto-correlation-attention block
output: trend + seasonal recombined.
Auto-correlation attention:
  R = FFT-based autocorrelation of Q and K
  pick top-k lags
  aggregate values by rolling them by those lags and weighting by R
Complexity: O(L log L)
  • Strengths: decomposition as inductive bias is a real win on strongly seasonal series; autocorrelation captures period-aware structure.
  • Weaknesses: top-k autocorrelation is a heuristic; loses to PatchTST on benchmarks where seasonality is irregular.
  • When to pick: textbook seasonal series (utility load, retail, weather) with clean periodicity.

4 · FEDformer (Zhou et al. 2022)

Push attention further into the frequency domain. Frequency-Enhanced Decomposition: each token sequence is mapped via FFT, attention is applied in frequency space on a small subset of frequencies (low or randomly sampled), then mapped back. The intuition: most TS signals are sparse in the frequency domain — a few dominant frequencies carry most of the predictable energy. 

For each layer:
  trend_t, seasonal_t = decompose(input)
  seasonal_t -> FFT
  attention applied on a random or low-frequency subset
  inverse FFT
  recombine with trend
Complexity: O(L) when frequency subset is fixed-size.
  • Strengths: O(L) attention on the frequency subset; clean for periodic-dominated signals.
  • Weaknesses: random-frequency-subset attention is a strong prior — if your signal has a non-stationary spectrum (regime shift, holiday-driven mixing), you lose; later variants (PatchTST) match or beat it without the FFT machinery.
  • When to pick: explicit frequency-domain knowledge (seasonal load, audio, vibration). Niche for airline pricing.

5 · PatchTST (Nie et al. 2023)

Did two surprisingly effective things and beat all the previous fancy variants. First, patch tokens: group every 16 timepoints into a token. Sequence length L = 720 becomes ~45 tokens; quadratic attention is now affordable. (TimesFM reused this trick.) Second, channel independence: forecast each variate using the same transformer weights but on its own forward pass. The model never sees other variates' values. 

Per channel, independently:
  x_c[1..L] -> patches of length 16 -> embed -> transformer -> head -> y_c[L+1..L+H]
All channels share the same transformer weights.
Complexity: O((L/16)^2) attention per channel.
  • Strengths: empirically beats Autoformer / FEDformer on standard TS benchmarks; channel independence is more robust under spurious correlation; trivial to fine-tune.
  • Weaknesses: doesn't exploit cross-channel signal at all; if your variates are densely correlated (energy + temperature) you leave information on the table.
  • When to pick: ETS-like univariate or weakly-coupled multivariate; one panel, deep history. Also: it's the baseline TimesFM, Chronos-Bolt, and Moirai-MoE all beat or match.

6 · Crossformer (Zhang & Yan 2023)

The opposite philosophy from PatchTST: explicitly model both cross-time and cross-variate dependencies. Two attention stages: cross-time attention within each variate (like PatchTST), then cross-variate attention across variates at each time-patch position. A learned router module decides how much of each. 

Per layer:
  Stage 1 (cross-time):    attention over patches within each variate
  Stage 2 (cross-variate): attention over variates within each patch position
Complexity: O((L/p)^2 + D^2) per layer for L sequence, D variates.
  • Strengths: captures dense cross-variate signal that PatchTST loses; competitive when correlations are real.
  • Weaknesses: more parameters; D2 cross-variate attention scales poorly with channel count; in low-data regimes the cross-variate attention overfits.
  • When to pick: dense multivariate (energy + weather + occupancy), < 50 channels, deep history.

7 · iTransformer (Liu et al. 2024)

Inverts the token axis. Where every previous variant had one token-per-timestep (or per-patch), iTransformer has one token-per-variate. Each variate's entire history becomes a single embedded vector; attention is applied across variates. Feed-forward network operates per-variate-token to learn variate-specific non-linearities. 

For one forward pass:
  x[1..L, 1..D] (L timesteps, D variates)
  -> embed each variate as a token: tokens[1..D, d_model]
  -> transformer attends over the D variate tokens
  -> per-variate FFN
  -> per-variate forecast head
Complexity: O(D^2) attention; L is hidden inside the embedding step.
  • Strengths: empirically the best transformer on multivariate benchmarks (ICLR 2024 Spotlight). Lookback length L is decoupled from attention cost; longer lookbacks “just work.”
  • Weaknesses: scales as O(D2); hits a wall around D = 100 variates; per-variate forecast head means cold-start variates are unlearnable.
  • When to pick: small-D multivariate (D ≤ 50), deep lookback (L = years), one panel. Strong for an energy/weather/finance shop with 5–50 variates.

If we couldn't use a foundation model, what would we use?

  1. Domestic mature panel (~80 routes, 5+ years history): PatchTST per family. ~5.6% MAPE on the mature subset, ~0.5 pp worse than TimesFM but $0 in pretraining cost.
  2. International dense-multivariate (route + competitor + currency + weather): iTransformer. Lookback can be 5+ years without attention cost.
  3. Cold-start subset (28% of routes, < 90 days): nothing transformer-based works. Fall back to Prophet or a per-family hierarchical Bayes.
  4. Strongly seasonal demand (e.g. holiday-driven leisure routes): Autoformer. Decomposition inductive bias helps when seasonality is the dominant signal.

This is the alternative-architecture playbook the team would run if AA decided to fully own the model. Cost: ~6 months of engineering, $400k in compute, and we'd still lose on cold-start. We chose to use a foundation model for the same reasons most teams do.

Interview Q&A — transformer variants

Why did PatchTST beat Autoformer and FEDformer?
Two reasons. (1) Patching turned out to be a better attention reduction than ProbSparse / autocorrelation / FFT — it's simpler, makes the attention budget predictable, and lets the model learn its own decomposition rather than imposing one. (2) Channel-independence was robust under spurious correlation, which the joint-channel embeddings of earlier variants suffered from.
If patching is the win, why is iTransformer better than PatchTST on multivariate benchmarks?
Because patching forces a tradeoff between lookback length and attention cost. iTransformer breaks that tradeoff: lookback length is hidden inside the per-variate embedding, attention is purely across variates. So with D = 20 variates and L = 5 years of daily data, attention cost is O(202) regardless of L. That's a real advantage for finance / energy / weather panels with deep history.
When is iTransformer wrong?
When D is large (D > 100) the D2 attention falls apart. When variates are weakly coupled, the cross-variate attention overfits to noise. And when you have cold-start variates, the per-variate forecast head is unlearnable — iTransformer needs every variate to have history.
Walk me through Autoformer's autocorrelation attention.
Compute the autocorrelation of Q and K via FFT — this is fast (O(L log L)). Pick the top-k lags by autocorrelation strength; these are the “periods” the model attends to. Aggregate values by rolling them by those lags, weighted by autocorrelation. The intuition: classical TS uses ACF to find seasonality; Autoformer makes that the attention pattern. Powerful when seasonality is clean; less so when it's irregular.
Why doesn't FEDformer crush every benchmark, given how parameter-efficient it is?
Two reasons. (1) The random-frequency-subset attention is a strong prior; when the signal has a non-stationary spectrum (regime shift, holiday mixing), you systematically lose. (2) On benchmarks dominated by trend or sudden events rather than periodic structure, FFT attention has nothing to attend to. PatchTST's “learn your own patterns from patches” turned out to be more robust.
How does Informer's ProbSparse attention compare to standard sparse attention?
Standard sparse attention drops fixed slots (Reformer LSH, Longformer windowed). ProbSparse drops queries based on KL-divergence-from-uniform — queries that are spread evenly across keys (close to uniform attention) get dropped, on the theory that they're uninformative. It's a heuristic; later work showed it's no better than uniform sparse attention plus more layers.
Why isn't Crossformer the obvious answer for AA pricing if we have route + competitor + fuel + holiday channels?
Three reasons. (1) Many of those channels are static (fuel hedge price, holiday flag) — they don't need cross-time attention. (2) The AA-confidential cross-route signal is captured by the LoRA family adapter, not by joint attention. (3) Cross-variate D2 attention in Crossformer overfits when channel correlations are noisy or non-stationary, which our competitor-fare signal is.
If you had to ship without foundation models, which variant would you pick?
PatchTST per route family. ~5.6% MAPE on our mature subset (vs 5.1% for TimesFM), < $1k/route family pretraining, channel-independence is robust, the eval harness is straightforward. The 0.5 pp gap is acceptable; the 28% cold-start population is the unsolvable problem — for that we'd fall back to Prophet or a hierarchical Bayes.
Why did the field go from variant-explosion to foundation models?
Each variant added an inductive bias to fix one problem. Foundation models removed the need for inductive bias by scaling pretraining: with 2 T tokens of diverse TS, the model learned its own decomposition, autocorrelation, and frequency structure. The variants are still useful when you can't pretrain (private data, audit, edge), but for cross-domain transfer, pretraining beat architectural cleverness.
What would you build if you had to do TS on a new domain tomorrow?
Step 1: try TimesFM zero-shot. Cheap baseline. Step 2: if cold-start is > 20% of the panel, stay with TimesFM + LoRA family adapters. Step 3: if you have one fixed panel with 5+ years of history and no cold-start, switch to PatchTST per panel — cheaper, owned-model audit story, < 1 pp accuracy gap. Step 4: if that panel is small-D multivariate (D ≤ 50) with dense correlations, switch to iTransformer instead. The decision tree is pretty stable.
