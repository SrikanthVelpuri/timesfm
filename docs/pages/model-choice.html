Why TimesFM 2.5? — the model-choice trade-off

For 6,700 OD-pairs × 30-day horizon × 8 dynamic + 3 static covariates, we evaluated 9 candidate model families before committing to TimesFM 2.5. This page is the comparison matrix and the reasoning, written so I can recall it cold in an interview.

TL;DR. Picked TimesFM 2.5 because it is the only candidate that simultaneously: (a) handles the long tail of OD-pairs with <90 days history via its zero-shot foundation prior, (b) supports calibrated quantile output without post-hoc bootstrapping, (c) accepts the AA covariate stack through forecast_with_covariates(), and (d) ships as a single 200 M-param artifact that fits on one A10G. ARIMAX (status quo) cold-starts badly. Prophet has weak quantiles. DeepAR/NBEATS need per-group training. Chronos lacks a covariate API. LLMs are wrong tool. Result: 10.2% → 5.1% MAPE network-wide.

Why TimesFM and not Chronos (the closest competitor)

Chronos was the most painful "no" because the model is genuinely good. Three reasons we picked TimesFM:

  1. Native covariate API. Chronos forecasts univariate; if you want covariates you have to bolt on your own residual regression. TimesFM ships forecast_with_covariates() with the regression layer baked in. Saves us building and maintaining an ad-hoc XReg layer.
  2. Quantile head architecture. Chronos uses sampling-based quantiles (run K samples, take percentiles) which is K× slower at inference and badly calibrated in the tails. TimesFM has a deterministic quantile head with optional fix_quantile_crossing=True.
  3. Smaller wins on inference cost. Chronos ships 200 M / 710 M / 2.7 B variants. The 200 M variant matches TimesFM's footprint but underperforms on accuracy; the 710 M variant matches accuracy but at 3.5× the inference cost. TimesFM 2.5 200 M is the Pareto winner.

Why not just stay with ARIMAX (the lazy answer)

Reasons ARIMAX is appealing

  • Already in production; team knows it.
  • Coefficients are interpretable.
  • Fast per-OD-pair inference.

Why we replaced it anyway

  • ~28% of OD-pairs have <90 days stable history; ARIMAX cold-starts to a global mean — bad for new routes / re-timed seasonals.
  • 6,700 model artifacts in the registry; impossible to monitor or audit.
  • Bootstrap PIs are ~80% miscalibrated in production; coverage was 71% (vs 80% target).
  • Q4 holiday MAPE was 14% — the per-route ARIMAX never sees enough Thanksgivings to fit the holiday term well.
  • Adding a new covariate requires re-fitting all 6,700 models. Operational nightmare.

Why not GPT-style LLM with tool use?

A surprisingly common interview suggestion. Short answer: it is the wrong architecture for time-series. Long answer:

  • Tokenization of real-valued series via digit-by-digit (Chronos-style) or quantile-binning is lossy; LLMs don't know what "$312.47" is, they see characters.
  • Calibration is essentially absent — LLMs do not produce probability distributions over real numbers.
  • Inference cost is 100–1000× foundation TS models per call.
  • Tool-use orchestration (e.g., agent calls Prophet, then aggregates) just hides a smaller model inside a bigger system — you might as well call the smaller model directly.
Interview hook: This is the question every senior interviewer asks. The framing they want: "right tool for the right scale of problem." LLMs are great at sequence modelling over discrete tokens; time series foundation models are great at sequence modelling over continuous patches. They are different families with different inductive biases.

Decision criteria, weighted

How I scored the candidates. Weights are AA-team-specific, not universal.

CriterionWeightWhy this weight
Cold-start handling25%28% of inventory has <90 d history; the legacy system fails here
Calibrated quantiles20%The C++ optimizer requires q10/q90 with monotonicity guarantee
Covariate API20%8 dynamic + 3 static covariates is the project's core differentiator
Operational simplicity15%One artifact vs 6,700; auditability for compliance
Inference cost10%Nightly batch must complete in <90 s on one A10G
License + community5%Apache-2.0 is required by AA legal for commercial use
MAPE point estimate5%Lower weight than expected — calibration matters more than median accuracy
Insight worth remembering: point-MAPE is over-emphasized in academic papers and under-emphasized by RMS optimizers. The optimizer needs distributions, not means. A model with 6.0% MAPE and well-calibrated 80% PI is better than a model with 5.5% MAPE and 65% coverage on the same band — the second model would burn revenue when the optimizer trusted bid-prices that were over-confident.

Risks I called out at decision time

  • Foundation-model lock-in to Google. Mitigation: Chronos is a credible second source; our XReg head + LoRA layer is portable.
  • License changes. Apache-2.0 today, but Google could relicense future versions. Mitigation: pin the 2.5 weights at the version we ship; do not auto-upgrade.
  • Calibration drift after fine-tune. Mitigation: held-out calibration check in CI; coverage is a release gate.
  • Distributional shift on COVID-style regimes. Mitigation: train data excludes COVID; production monitor will page on coverage drop > 5 pp.

What I would do differently

  • Run Chronos+XReg-myself ablation in week 1, not "trust vendor benchmarks". Two models from the same family of architectures might actually behave very differently on AA data.
  • Include a parametric-output baseline (e.g., DeepAR with negative-binomial likelihood) in the matrix; I dropped it for time and would re-add it next time as a calibration sanity check.

Interview Q&A

Why did you pick a foundation model over a per-route specialized model?
28% of our OD-inventory has <90 days stable history. Per-route fits cold-start to a global mean and bleed Q4 revenue. A foundation model has an implicit prior from 100B time-series points that gives reasonable forecasts on day-1 of a new OD-pair. We capture the AA-specific patterns through cheap fine-tuning (XReg head + LoRA), so we get the foundation prior and AA-specialization without giving up either.
How do you know TimesFM's 80% prediction intervals are actually calibrated?
Two checks. (1) Held-out coverage on the val cohort: the fraction of actuals falling between q10 and q90 should be ~80%; ours is 79.3%. (2) Reliability diagram: predicted vs realized coverage at every quantile from 10% to 90%. Both are part of the CI gate — coverage outside [78%, 82%] blocks the model release. We also sanity-check holiday-day coverage separately because that's where calibration usually breaks.
Could you have just used XGBoost with engineered features instead?
For point forecasts on routes with long history, XGBoost would do well — probably comparable point MAPE. Two reasons we did not: (a) probabilistic output requires quantile regression with K separate XGB models, expensive at scale and miscalibrated relative to a learned quantile head; (b) cold-start performance on new OD-pairs is poor — XGB has no cross-route prior unless we engineer one through embeddings, which is essentially re-implementing the foundation model badly.
What would change if you had only 100 OD-pairs instead of 6,700?
Inverts most of the trade-offs. Per-route NBEATS or DeepAR becomes attractive because the operational cost of 100 artifacts is fine. Foundation-model overhead amortizes worse. I would still use TimesFM zero-shot as a baseline, but the fine-tuning ROI is not there at 100 series.
What is the upper bound on accuracy if you had unlimited budget?
Honestly somewhere around 4.0–4.3% network MAPE. Full fine-tune of the trunk would get us to ~4.7% (we measured). Beyond that you would need (a) more covariates, especially booking-velocity features that are harder to feed without leakage, and (b) a hierarchical reconciliation layer so route forecasts sum coherently to network totals. The marginal cost of going from 5.1% to 4.0% is >100× the cost of going from 10.2% to 5.1%, so I would not invest there until the easy wins on adjacent problems are exhausted.
Walk me through the one paragraph in the TimesFM paper that mattered most for your project.
The patching section. TimesFM groups every 32 input points into a "patch" then runs causal attention over the patch sequence. The patch-level attention (a) reduces sequence length so 90-day context is 3 patches not 90 tokens, (b) makes the model robust to single-day spikes because the patch averages context, and (c) lets the output head emit a 128-step patch, so we get our 30-day horizon in one forward pass without autoregressive sampling. That last bit is what makes the inference cheap enough for AA.
