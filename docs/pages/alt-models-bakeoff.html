Alt-models bake-off — the layers around TimesFM

TimesFM is the right tool for the forecast layer. It is the wrong tool for elasticity estimation, base-price optimization, and competitor reaction. This page is the honest model bake-off for those three adjacent problems, plus a sanity check on whether a different foundation model (TFT, TiDE, Chronos-Bolt, N-HiTS, DeepAR, PatchTST, Moirai-MoE) would have beaten TimesFM at our footprint.

TL;DR. Forecasting layer: TimesFM still wins at 6,700 routes with 28% cold-start; TFT/TiDE win at 100 mature routes. Elasticity layer: LightGBM with random effects (GPBoost) is the workhorse; Double ML / DeepIV when you need causal identification. Base-price layer: MIP + offline Bayesian-opt is the safe pick; contextual bandits belong on the per-request bot, not on the strategic anchor. Competitor layer: covariate approach is mis-specified; Stackelberg / inverse optimization on the top-20 competitive ODs is the next research bet.

For the 100-route panel specifically, TFT and TiDE are the closest competitors if you don't have the foundation-model option. TFT learns interpretable variable selection per series — valuable when a regulator wants to know which covariate drove a forecast. TiDE is a pure encoder MLP that's faster than TFT on long horizons. Both require per-panel training, so both lose decisively on cold-start (28% of our 6,700 routes have < 90 days of clean history). Cite: Lim et al., International Journal of Forecasting, 2021 for TFT; Das et al., arXiv 2304.08424 for TiDE.

If the problem were just 100 mature routes with 3+ years of clean history and no cold-start, TFT or TiDE+covariates would be in serious contention. With 6,700 routes and 28% cold-start, TimesFM still wins.

Layer 2 — Elasticity estimation (NOT a forecasting problem)

Elasticity is a causal parameter — the partial derivative of demand with respect to price, holding everything else fixed. Forecasting models recover predictive coefficients confounded with policy. This is a different problem and needs a different toolbox.

Honesty edit. The XReg head's price coefficient is not a causal elasticity. It is a regression coefficient on observational data fit under the existing AA pricing policy — it picks up demand-driven price moves and policy responses, conflated. We label it elast in metrics.json for shorthand, but the base-price optimizer should consume a separately-estimated causal elasticity (Double ML / DeepIV / hierarchical Bayes with an instrument). The OR team owns this layer.

The honest stack: LightGBM + categorical features as the workhorse for price-response prediction; GPBoost or hierarchical Bayes when partial pooling matters (low-volume routes); Double ML or DeepIV when you need causal elasticity for the optimizer. Most teams pick LightGBM and call it “elasticity”; it isn't, unless you control for confounding. Be honest about which model is doing what.

Layer 3 — Base-price optimization

Once you have a forecast distribution and an elasticity, the optimizer chooses a base price that maximises expected revenue subject to constraints (floor, ceiling, ladder smoothness, fare-class minima, brand-positioning rules).

For AA's compliance environment, MIP with offline Bayesian-opt search of constraint settings is the safe pick. Contextual bandits are the right pick for the dynamic-pricing bot (Layer 4), where the per-request decision can absorb exploration noise. Don't put a bandit at the strategic layer — you cannot explain a strategic price to legal as “we were exploring.”

Layer 4 — Competitor reaction

Forecasting competitor pricing is one thing; modelling how they react to your price is another. Naive forecasting treats competitor fare as exogenous; in equilibrium it is endogenous. This is where most airline RM systems quietly cheat.

Stay with the covariate approach for v1; flag this as known mis-specification. We quantified the bias from a small Stackelberg simulation: 1.2 pp MAPE inflation on the most-competed 20 routes. Acceptable given the operational complexity. Adding a Stackelberg leader-follower model on those 20 ODs is the right next bet.

The honest summary

TimesFM is right for the forecast layer. The portfolio understated this by not explicitly saying what TimesFM is not the right tool for. For elasticity, base price, and competitor reaction, the right tool is not a foundation TS model. It is a different model class, owned by a different team, with a different update cadence and different audit requirements. The stacked architecture — one forecaster + one elasticity estimator + one optimizer + one bot — makes that explicit and makes each layer separately reviewable.

The user's instinct on the original prompt was correct. LightGBM / CatBoost + elasticity + optimizer is the right base-price stack. TimesFM contributes the demand forecast at the top of that stack and nothing else. The bot operates at the bottom and personalises around the strategic anchor. Each layer's job is bounded.

Interview Q&A — alt-models bake-off

Is there a better model than TimesFM for your forecasting layer?
Not at our footprint. TFT and TiDE are credible if we had only mature routes with no cold-start. Chronos-Bolt is the closest foundation-model competitor — we benchmarked at 5.9% network MAPE vs TimesFM's 5.1%. We re-evaluate quarterly; Moirai-MoE is on the watch list.
What's the difference between the XReg head's price coefficient and a true elasticity?
Confounding. The XReg coefficient is fit on observational data where price was set by current policy; it picks up demand-driven price moves and policy responses, conflated. A true elasticity needs an instrument (Double ML, DeepIV) or randomised variation. The portfolio labels it elast for shorthand; the optimizer should consume a separately-estimated causal elasticity from the OR team.
Why GPBoost over plain LightGBM for elasticity?
GPBoost adds Gaussian random effects per group (route, route family); it shrinks elasticity estimates on low-volume routes toward the family mean. Sigrist 2022 shows it dominates LightGBM on grouped data. The downside is it's slower to fit and the API is less mature, but for elasticity at 6,700 routes the partial-pooling story is worth it.
Why not put a bandit on the base price?
Strategic prices need to be explained to legal and revenue management. Bandits explore. The combination is bad — you cannot tell a director “we set DFW-LAX's anchor at $180 this week because our bandit was exploring.” Bandits belong on the per-request bot (L4), where the decision can absorb the exploration noise; not on the weekly strategic anchor.
Walk me through Double ML for fare elasticity.
Two ML models: one predicts demand from non-price features, one predicts price from non-price features. Cross-fit residuals; regress demand-residual on price-residual; the coefficient is a debiased elasticity. Robust to confounding via Neyman orthogonality. Chernozhukov et al. 2018. Tooling: econml, doubleml in Python.
When would you actually pick TFT over TimesFM?
100 well-known routes, 3+ years of stable history, hard requirement for per-feature variable-selection interpretability for a regulator. Lim et al. designed TFT for that audience. We don't fit any of those constraints — 6,700 routes, 28% cold-start, regulator wants signed artefacts not feature-attribution — so TimesFM wins.
How do you handle 6,700 routes for elasticity when most have < 500 days history?
Hierarchical Bayes (Stan/PyMC) or GPBoost. Random effects per route nested in family priors; shrinkage toward family means is automatic. Plain per-route OLS is wildly noisy on < 500 days. We don't do this today (OR team owns the layer); the page recommends it.
What's wrong with using booking outcomes to retrain the forecast?
Bookings reflect accepted prices, not the demand curve. Training on realised bookings contracts the model toward the policy you ran — same lesson as the weather scenario page where we train on scheduled, not realised, irrops flags.
Why is competitor fare treated as exogenous if it isn't?
Pragmatic. The right answer is multi-agent or inverse optimization. We flagged it as known mis-specification and quantified the bias from a small Stackelberg simulation: 1.2 pp MAPE inflation on the most-competed 20 routes. Acceptable given the operational complexity; the next research bet is a Stackelberg leader-follower model on those 20 ODs.
Causal forests vs Double ML for elasticity heterogeneity?
Causal forests give per-route treatment effects with valid CIs (Wager & Athey 2018) but assume unconfoundedness or have to be combined with instruments. Double ML is more flexible on the nuisance side; less natural for heterogeneity. We'd use causal forests if we had a credible instrument and wanted per-OD elasticity directly with confidence intervals.
