Hierarchical forecasting — reconciling 6,700 routes with one network total

Airlines forecast at multiple levels of aggregation: network total revenue, regional totals, route family, individual OD-pair, fare cabin. These forecasts must add up — sum of route forecasts equals the regional total. Independent forecasts at each level violate this. Hierarchical reconciliation is the machinery that fixes it. This page is the deep-dive of the techniques (BU, TD, MO, OLS, MinT) and what we ship at AA.

TL;DR. Five reconciliation methods. Bottom-up: sum the leaves; coherent by construction but loses high-level signal. Top-down: forecast the root, disaggregate by historical proportions; great at the top, fragile at the leaves. Middle-out: forecast at family level, reconcile up and down. OLS reconciliation: minimum-variance unbiased reconciliation under independence assumption. MinT (Wickramasuriya et al. 2019): the optimal-trace-minimisation estimator that uses the full forecast-error covariance — the gold standard. We ship MinT(Shrink) post-hoc on top of TimesFM forecasts, gaining ~0.6 pp network-level MAPE while preserving per-OD fidelity.

Five levels: network → region → family → OD → cabin. Capacity planning lives at the network and regional levels; revenue management lives at the OD and cabin levels. Both levels need forecasts; both need to be coherent.

Why coherence matters

Three concrete problems with incoherent forecasts:

  1. Capacity planning. If sum(OD forecasts for region) > regional capacity but the regional forecast is below capacity, revenue planners and operations get conflicting signals.
  2. Cross-route attribution. If DFW-LAX forecast moves +2% but the LA-basin family forecast is flat, where did the difference go? Reconciliation answers cleanly.
  3. Aggregation in reporting. Board-level reports sum sub-totals. Incoherent forecasts mean the sum doesn't equal the published top-line. Audit nightmare.

The independent-forecast solution (forecast every level separately, ignore the inconsistency) is what most teams do by default. Hierarchical reconciliation is the discipline that fixes it.

The reconciliation problem — formal setup

Let y_t be the vector of all observations across the hierarchy at time t (network total, regional totals, family totals, OD, cabin) — for AA that's ~7,500 series. Let S be the summation matrix that says “sum the leaves to get parents.” Then y_t = S * b_t where b_t are the leaf-level bottom series.

A base forecast y_hat_t is produced by forecasting each level independently. It's incoherent: y_hat ≠ S * (leaves of y_hat). A reconciled forecast tilde_y_t = S * P * y_hat_t is coherent (sums up correctly) by construction; the problem is finding the right P matrix.

1 · Bottom-Up (BU)

Forecast only the leaves; aggregate to higher levels by summation. P picks out the leaf entries of y_hat and zeros the rest. 

For each OD-cabin (leaf):
    base_forecast_leaf = TimesFM(history_leaf)
For each parent level (family, region, network):
    reconciled = sum of leaf forecasts under that parent
  • Pros: coherent by construction; uses leaf-level information directly; simple.
  • Cons: leaf-level noise compounds at the top; loses any high-level structure (network seasonality, holiday-driven aggregate effects); can't handle situations where the aggregate is more accurately forecastable than the parts (which is common).
  • When to pick: when leaves are well-forecasted and aggregates aren't additionally informative. Default if you do nothing.

2 · Top-Down (TD)

Forecast only the network total; disaggregate down by historical proportions. P spreads the network forecast onto the leaves. 

base_forecast_network = TimesFM(network history)
For each leaf:
    proportion_leaf = historical share of leaf in network
    reconciled_leaf = base_forecast_network * proportion_leaf
For all intermediate levels: sum the leaves under each parent
  • Pros: very accurate at the top level; one model is enough; smooth.
  • Cons: assumes proportions are stable, which they are not for new routes, seasonal shifts, or competitive entry; produces biased forecasts at the leaf level (Hyndman et al. 2011 proved this).
  • When to pick: when leaves are too noisy to forecast individually and proportions are stable.

3 · Middle-Out (MO)

Forecast at an intermediate level (route family), aggregate up and disaggregate down. Compromise between BU and TD.

For AA, family-level forecasting is well-supported by ~20 family LoRAs. Middle-out at the family level gives reasonable accuracy at both ends but still uses historical proportions for the family-to-leaf split, inheriting TD's leaf-bias problem.

4 · OLS Reconciliation (Hyndman et al. 2011)

Forecast independently at every level, then reconcile by least-squares projection onto the coherence constraint. 

# Choose P that minimizes ||tilde_y - y_hat||^2 subject to coherence
# Closed-form: P = (S' S)^-1 S'
# Reconciled forecast:
tilde_y = S * (S' S)^-1 S' * y_hat

Equivalent to projecting y_hat onto the column space of S in Euclidean distance. Unbiased under independence + identical variance assumption (which is rarely true). Easy to compute, no covariance estimation needed.

  • Pros: closed-form; uses all base forecasts; unbiased under independence.
  • Cons: ignores forecast-error correlations; equal weighting wastes high-confidence forecasts.
  • When to pick: as a baseline before you have a covariance estimate; when independence is plausible.

5 · MinT (Minimum Trace, Wickramasuriya et al. 2019)

The optimal reconciliation. P is chosen to minimise the trace of the reconciled-forecast-error covariance, subject to the coherence constraint. Closed-form solution depends on the base-forecast error covariance W: 

P_MinT = (S' W^-1 S)^-1 S' W^-1
tilde_y = S * P_MinT * y_hat

# W is the (Lh*Lh) error covariance of base forecasts at horizon h.
# Three common choices:
#   - MinT(WLS):    W = diag(MSE per series)               -- variance-weighted
#   - MinT(Sample): W = sample covariance from holdout
#   - MinT(Shrink): W = shrinkage estimator toward diagonal
# Empirically MinT(Shrink) wins.
  • Pros: provably best linear reconciliation under the variance-trace criterion; uses all base forecasts AND their correlations; handles heterogeneous variance correctly.
  • Cons: requires an estimate of the base-forecast error covariance W; W is high-dimensional (~7,500 series for AA) so shrinkage is needed.
  • When to pick: any production hierarchical-forecasting setup of meaningful size. Our pick.

How AA does it

# Step 1. Generate base forecasts at every level.
for level in [network, region, family, OD, cabin]:
    base[level] = TimesFM(level_history)

# Step 2. Estimate base-forecast error covariance W from holdout.
#         For 7,500 series, full sample covariance is rank-deficient;
#         use Schafer-Strimmer shrinkage toward a diagonal target.
W_shrink = shrinkage_cov(holdout_residuals)

# Step 3. Compute P_MinT once per quarter.
P = inv(S.T @ inv(W_shrink) @ S) @ S.T @ inv(W_shrink)

# Step 4. Reconcile every nightly forecast cycle.
tilde_y = S @ P @ base_y_hat

# Step 5. Audit: report base forecasts AND reconciled forecasts.
#         RM consumes reconciled; capacity planning consumes reconciled;
#         model debugging consumes base.

Effect on our metrics:

  • Network MAPE: 4.2% (base) → 3.6% (MinT). 0.6 pp improvement.
  • Regional MAPE: 4.8% → 4.3%. 0.5 pp.
  • Family MAPE: 5.1% → 4.9%. 0.2 pp.
  • OD MAPE: 5.1% → 5.1% (essentially unchanged; reconciliation is a small adjustment at the leaf).

The reason MinT improves the top more than the bottom: the base-forecast error correlations are highest among related series (DFW-LAX and DFW-LGB share many drivers); MinT exploits this to reduce variance at aggregated levels.

Probabilistic reconciliation

Point reconciliation is solved. Probabilistic reconciliation — how do you reconcile distributions, not just point forecasts — is active research.

Approaches:

  • Sample-and-reconcile. Draw S samples from each base distribution; reconcile each sample point-wise; take empirical quantiles of reconciled samples. Cheap and works in practice. Coverage is approximate.
  • Score-optimal reconciliation (Panagiotelis et al. 2023). Choose the reconciled distribution that minimises CRPS or energy score. More principled but tooling immature.
  • Bootstrap reconciliation. Bootstrap residuals at every level; reconcile each bootstrap; report empirical quantiles. Robust but expensive.

We use sample-and-reconcile in production: 1000 forecast paths from TimesFM's quantile head × nightly reconciliation + per-route quantile recovery. Costs ~$10/quarter in compute and gives reasonable PI coverage.

What goes wrong without hierarchical reconciliation

  1. The audit-summary mismatch. The board sees a network-level revenue forecast of $1.62B; the sum of OD forecasts says $1.59B. Auditor asks why; nobody has a clean answer.
  2. Conflicting capacity vs RM signals. Network forecast says ample capacity; sum of OD forecasts says oversold. Operations is left to guess.
  3. Lost information at the top. Network-level seasonality (Q4 holiday surge across all regions) is well-modelled at the top; bottom-up sums route-level forecasts that each underestimate it slightly, compounding.
  4. Spurious cross-route signal. Without reconciliation, two ODs that should move together (substitutes) move independently in the forecasts. Reconciliation correlates them through the parent level.

Failure modes

  1. Rank-deficient covariance. With 7,500 series and 90 days of holdout, the sample covariance W is rank-deficient. Mitigation: shrinkage estimator (Schafer-Strimmer, Ledoit-Wolf).
  2. Hierarchy misspecification. A route is in the wrong family; reconciliation pulls it the wrong way. Mitigation: per-quarter audit of family membership; hierarchy is a registered artefact.
  3. Drift in W. Forecast error structure changes (a new ULCC entry shifts variance on competitive routes). Mitigation: trigger W refresh on major structural events; otherwise quarterly.
  4. Negative reconciled forecasts. MinT can produce negative leaves on count data. Mitigation: log-transform before reconciliation, exponentiate after.
  5. Probabilistic intervals over-reconciled. Sample-and-reconcile can collapse PI bands at the leaves because intra-region correlations push samples together. Mitigation: monitor coverage; recalibrate via conformal post-reconciliation if needed.

Interview Q&A — hierarchical forecasting

Why bother with reconciliation at all?
Independent forecasts at every level are incoherent — the sum of leaf forecasts doesn't equal the parent forecast. That breaks audit, breaks capacity planning, and breaks aggregation in reporting. Reconciliation enforces coherence and, with MinT, also reduces variance at higher levels by exploiting forecast-error correlations.
Walk me through MinT.
Choose the reconciliation matrix P that minimises the trace of the reconciled-forecast-error covariance, subject to coherence. Closed-form: P = (S′ W-1 S)-1 S′ W-1, where W is the base-forecast error covariance and S is the summation matrix. Three common W choices: WLS uses diag(MSE), Sample uses sample covariance, Shrink uses shrinkage toward diagonal. Empirically MinT(Shrink) is best in production.
Why does Top-Down fail at the leaves?
Top-Down forecasts the root and disaggregates by historical proportions. Hyndman et al. 2011 showed this produces biased leaf-level forecasts even when the top forecast is unbiased — because proportions are estimated, not exact, and the bias compounds at the leaves. For new routes, seasonal shifts, or competitive entry, proportions are also non-stationary, so TD fails twice.
When would you pick Bottom-Up over MinT?
When the base-forecast error covariance can't be reliably estimated — e.g. small panels with short holdout. BU is the safe default; MinT requires W to be well-estimated. Also: when leaves are clearly the most informative level and aggregates add no signal beyond summation.
How do you estimate W for 7,500 series?
Sample covariance is rank-deficient with only 90 days of holdout. Use a shrinkage estimator (Schafer-Strimmer or Ledoit-Wolf) that pulls the sample covariance toward a structured target (diagonal). The shrinkage parameter is chosen by cross-validation; tooling: sklearn.covariance.LedoitWolf or the equivalent in the hts R package.
What does reconciliation buy you in numbers?
For our 5-level hierarchy: network MAPE 4.2% → 3.6%, regional 4.8% → 4.3%, family 5.1% → 4.9%, OD 5.1% → 5.1% (essentially unchanged). The gain is concentrated at higher levels because MinT exploits the cross-leaf correlation structure to reduce aggregated-forecast variance.
How do you reconcile probabilistic forecasts?
Sample-and-reconcile: draw 1000 paths from each base distribution, reconcile each sample point-wise, take empirical quantiles of reconciled samples. Cheap, works in production, coverage is approximate. The principled alternative is score-optimal reconciliation (Panagiotelis et al. 2023) but tooling is research-grade.
Could you fold reconciliation into the model itself?
Yes, in principle — train TimesFM (or a successor) on a reconciliation-aware loss that penalises incoherence. Research-grade; nobody's shipped this in production yet. The post-hoc reconciliation we ship is decoupled from the model, which has audit advantages: model rolls back without affecting reconciliation logic and vice versa.
What if your hierarchy isn't a tree?
For grouped time series (e.g. ODs grouped by both region AND day-of-week), the structure is a DAG, not a tree. The S matrix gets more rows but the math is the same. MinT generalises directly. Hyndman's fable R package handles both.
Does reconciliation interact with conformal prediction?
Yes. Reconciled point forecasts have different (typically narrower) variance than base forecasts, so the conformity scores need to be recomputed on reconciled residuals. We do per-route conformal calibration after reconciliation: same per-route holdout, conformity scores from reconciled errors, qhat per route.
If your network forecast and your sum-of-leaves disagree by 5%, who wins?
Neither, or both, depending on stage. Pre-reconciliation, the disagreement is the signal — it tells you the model has structure to exploit at one level the other doesn't see. Post-reconciliation, MinT chooses the variance-minimising reconciled forecast that respects coherence; that's what RM and capacity consume. Disagreement > 5% is a flag to check the hierarchy and the W estimate.
