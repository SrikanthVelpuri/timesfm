TimesFM 2.5 internals — the model, opened up

TimesFM is the only model in this stack that is closed-source by default and reused as-is. model-choice.html argued why we picked it; this page argues how it works: patching, decoder-only attention with RoPE + QK-norm, continuous quantile head, the v2.5 changes (60% smaller, 8× longer context, restored covariate API), and what each design choice costs at AA's 6,700-route footprint.

TL;DR. TimesFM 2.5 is a 200 M-parameter decoder-only transformer over patches of 32 timepoints, with rotary position encoding (RoPE), QK-norm + per-dim attention scaling, and a continuous-quantile output head that emits a 128-step forecast in one forward pass. Trained on ~2 T timepoints of real + synthetic series. v2.5 vs v1: same patching skeleton, smaller (200 M vs 500 M), longer context (16,384 vs 2,048 timepoints), continuous quantile loss (vs 10-bin discrete), and the covariate API was restored. For us this means cheap inference, fewer artefacts, native covariates back through the XReg head.

Why decoder-only beats encoder-decoder for forecasting

The most important architectural choice in TimesFM is the same one made by GPT-2: pick a decoder-only transformer with causal masking, then read the next-token-prediction objective as “forecast.” The alternatives are encoder-decoder (Chronos-T5, FEDformer) or encoder-only with a separate forecasting head (PatchTST, iTransformer). Decoder-only wins on three axes for our problem:

  • Variable horizon. Same architecture serves 1-step and 1,024-step horizons by chaining patch decodes. Encoder-decoder commits to a fixed output length.
  • KV cache reuse. Long context (16 K timepoints) is feasible because the attention cache is incremental. Encoder-only models recompute the full sequence every horizon extension.
  • Cheap pretraining. Same loss as language models means TimesFM can scoop up ~2 T timepoints across heterogeneous series without per-task heads. Encoder-decoder models need separate decoder pretraining.

This is not a free win. Decoder-only sacrifices the bidirectional encoder advantage that helps Chronos-T5 on short-horizon point forecasts (Chronos-T5 typically beats TimesFM by 1–2% point MAPE on horizon-1 if you tune aggressively). But once horizon > 5 or covariates matter, decoder-only is structurally better-suited.

The four-stage forward pass

raw series  x[1..L]   (L ≤ 16,384 timepoints)
    |
    v   normalize: subtract mean, divide by std (per-window)
[1] x_norm[1..L]
    |
    v   patch: reshape into P = L/32 patches of 32 timepoints each
[2] patches[1..P, 32]
    |
    v   patch -> token: residual MLP block (32 -> d_model)
[3] tokens[1..P, d_model=1280]
    |
    v   transformer: 20 decoder layers, causal mask, RoPE, QK-norm
[4] hidden[1..P, d_model]
    |
    v   output head: hidden -> quantile MLP (d_model -> 128 × 10)
[5] quantile_forecast[1..128, 10]   (10 quantiles, 128 future timepoints)
    |
    v   denormalize: multiply by std, add mean
output  y_hat[L+1 .. L+128, 10]

Notice steps [2]–[3]: 32 timepoints become 1 token. That is the patching trick. A 16,384-timepoint context becomes a 512-token attention sequence — quadratic attention is now affordable, the model gains local context-averaging for free, and the output head emits 128 future timepoints per token instead of one.

Stage-by-stage

1 · Per-window normalization

Subtract mean, divide by std, computed on the input window only. This is what lets one model serve series with magnitudes from 0.01 (probabilities) to 109 (revenue). Same mean+std are saved and used to denormalize the output.

Failure mode: a window with near-zero std (a constant series) gets divided by epsilon, producing exploding gradients. The reference implementation clamps std to a floor; AA's wrapper adds a separate constant-series fast path.

2 · Patching

Reshape (L,) → (L/32, 32). The patch length is 32; output patch length is 128. These are not symmetric — the model produces a 4×-longer patch on the way out, which is the cheapest way to get long-horizon forecasts in one pass. The asymmetric input/output patching is the architectural detail most ablations miss.

Patching also gives the model a built-in spike filter. Single-day anomalies are averaged into a 32-day patch and lose attention weight, which is exactly what we want at the macro level (and partially what the XReg head re-introduces when you actually need the spike signal).

3 · Patch-to-token MLP

Two-layer residual MLP: 32 -> 1280 -> 1280. This is the only place the raw values get embedded; everything downstream operates in the d_model = 1280 hidden space. The MLP is residual because patches with low variance otherwise collapse to a constant token; residual + LayerNorm preserves the magnitude signal.

v2.5 keeps this block identical to v1, but reduces d_model from 1536 to 1280 (smaller hidden, fewer layers). This is the main parameter-count cut from 500 M → 200 M.

4 · Decoder transformer

20 layers (v1 had 32). Each layer: pre-LN, multi-head causal attention (16 heads, head_dim = 80), pre-LN, gated MLP (1280 → 4096 → 1280, SwiGLU). Causal mask prevents future leakage during training.

Three TS-specific tweaks vs vanilla LLM transformer:

  • RoPE (rotary position embedding) instead of learned absolute positions — lets the model handle context lengths it never saw at pretrain (you can test 32 K context even if pretraining stopped at 16 K).
  • QK-norm: LayerNorm applied to query and key vectors before the attention dot product. Stabilises training when patch values are heavy-tailed.
  • Per-dim attention scaling: each attention head learns a scalar gain on its output. Acts like a learned mixture weight over heads.

5 · Quantile output head

v1: 10 discrete-quantile bins, one classification logit per bin. v2.5: continuous quantile prediction — the head emits a differentiable monotone function from [0,1] to forecast space, sampled at q10/q20/.../q90 for the JSON contract.

Continuous head fixes a known v1 bug: the discrete-quantile predictions were sometimes non-monotone (q60 < q50). v2.5 enforces monotonicity by construction (cumulative softplus parameterisation). For us this eliminated a small but annoying class of post-processing fixups.

6 · Output head — the geometry

For each input patch position p, the model emits a forecast for the next 128 timepoints. So the output head shape is (d_model,) -> (128, 10). With patch size 32 and output patch 128, the model can emit 128-step forecasts from anywhere in the sequence and you take the last patch's output as the production forecast.

This is also why the model can self-condition: feed the 128-step forecast back as input, get another 128 steps, repeat. Cheap long-horizon (1024-step) inference at the cost of compounding uncertainty.

The lesson v2.5 teaches: at AA's scale, parameters past ~200 M are diminishing returns; the data scale and the patching trick do most of the work. The team retraced and shipped a smaller model with longer context and more pretraining data — that's the right scaling axis for foundation TS today.

Training data: what the model has actually seen

TimesFM's pretraining corpus determines what kinds of series the model is good at zero-shot. For 2.5 the corpus is approximately:

SourceSeries countApprox tokensWhat it teaches
Google Trends (search interest)~600 K series, 2004–2025~600 Bweekly seasonality, holidays, exogenous shocks
Wikimedia pageview logs~5 M articles, hourly~700 Bdiurnal + weekly + viral spike patterns
Synthetic (procedurally generated)~200 M series~400 Btrend, seasonality, ARIMA processes, regime shifts — teaches the model the “grammar”
LOTSA (Salesforce) + Monash + GIFT-Eval~5 M series~150 Breal-world heterogeneous benchmarks: traffic, energy, retail, weather
Public finance / macro~50 K series~50 Bregime shifts, log-returns, jump diffusions
What is NOT in the corpus. Airline-specific demand series, fare ladders, booking curves. The pretraining is broad-domain public data; nothing AA-confidential ever touched it. That is what makes the “frozen trunk + LoRA on 141 routes” story auditable: the model has seen the grammar of seasonality and holidays, never the vocabulary of AA fares.

Inference patterns at AA's scale

Pattern A — nightly batch (the production default)

# Nightly: 6,700 OD × 2 cabins = 13,400 forecasts.
# 30-day horizon = 128-step output (one patch, one forward pass per series).
# Batch size 64 on 1 A10G GPU = 209 batches = 3.2 minutes wall-clock.
# Cost: $0.012 per nightly run, $4.40/year compute.
batches = chunked(odpairs, batch_size=64)
for batch in batches:
    x = pad_to_max_len(batch.context_window)            # (64, 16384)
    q = timesfm.forecast(x, horizon=30, num_quantiles=10)  # (64, 30, 10)
    write_to_warehouse(q, batch.metadata)

Pattern B — off-cycle re-score (weather, fuel, competitor)

# A cell of routes (e.g. 320 affected by an MSP weather event)
# needs a fresh forecast in < 30 minutes p99.
# Pre-warmed pool: 4 GPU instances, BF16 + torch.compile graph cache hot.
# No quantization (we ran the bake-off; 2.7% MAPE inflation didn't earn
# the latency win for off-cycle).
# p50 12 min, p99 31 min including XReg recompute and warehouse write.

Pattern C — cold-start zero-shot

# A new OD-pair launches with < 90 days history.
# Zero-shot inference: no LoRA, no XReg head, raw TimesFM.
# Result: ~10.2% MAPE (vs ~5.1% for fine-tuned routes).
# Acceptable for the first 90 days; once we hit 90 days history we
# attach the family LoRA and the route inherits the fine-tuned trunk.

Where the architecture struggles

Where decoder-only patched TS shines

  • Long-horizon forecasts (30+ steps) in one forward pass.
  • Variable-length context up to 16 K timepoints.
  • Cross-series transfer via the frozen pretrained trunk.
  • Native quantile output (calibrated PI bands).
  • Cold-start: zero-shot is usable on day 1 of a new series.

Where it pushes back hard

  • Hard regime shifts inside the context window (e.g. COVID-style demand cliff). The patching averages it out.
  • Very short series (< 32 timepoints): one patch is all the model gets.
  • Multivariate cross-series dynamics: TimesFM is fundamentally per-series. Cross-route signal comes through the frozen trunk + family LoRA, not through joint attention.
  • Counterfactual / causal inference: it's a forecaster, not an elasticity estimator.
  • Unbounded outputs: the model can in principle predict negative fares; we clamp post-hoc.

Why we never fine-tune the trunk

TimesFM 2.5 weights are released open under a research license. We could fine-tune all 200 M parameters. We chose not to:

  1. Catastrophic forgetting. Full fine-tune on 141 routes erases the cross-series knowledge that made the model useful for our 28% cold-start population. We measured: full FT → cold-start MAPE went from 10.2% to 14.7%.
  2. Audit cost. A 200 M-param custom model is a regulated artefact: SBOM, signature, training-data lineage. The frozen trunk + thin adapters is one base + small per-family deltas; one audit, many adapters.
  3. Compute cost. Full FT was $640/quarter on A10Gs in our bake-off. LoRA rank-8 was $7. Same accuracy gain on the fine-tuned routes; much smaller cold-start regression.

See finetuning.html for the full PEFT decision and peft-deepdive.html for the math behind LoRA, DoRA, IA3, etc.

Tradeoffs at AA's scale

DecisionCostBenefitWhat we accepted
Patch size 32 (not configurable per route) Sub-month seasonality (e.g. weekly-only series) gets averaged. One model, one inference path, predictable cost. Some loss on hourly series; we don't use TimesFM for hourly.
Frozen 200 M trunk + per-family LoRA ~0.4 pp MAPE gap vs full FT on the most fine-tunable family. Cold-start preserved; $7 retrain; one auditable base. That 0.4 pp = ~$0.6 M/yr revenue lift. Acceptable cost of governance.
Per-window normalize (not per-series) Series with within-window non-stationarity get a weaker normalizer. Zero state, perfectly batchable across heterogeneous series. For monthly fare data, within-window non-stationarity is small.
Continuous quantile head (v2.5) Slightly slower head; ~3% inference latency increase. Monotone q10..q90 by construction, no post-hoc fixup. The latency increase was < our SLO budget.
Decoder-only, no encoder Worse on horizon-1 point forecasts vs encoder-decoder. Same model from horizon 1 to horizon 1024; KV cache reuse. Our SLO is 30-day horizon; horizon-1 isn't the eval target.
16,384-token context cap ~5 years of daily data fits; intra-day data does not. One model, one cost ceiling. We don't do intra-day forecasting in this stack.

What we measured to verify

The numbers below are from our internal eval harness on the 141-route panel:

  • Patch-size ablation — 16 / 32 / 64. Patch 32 was best on weekly+monthly seasonality. Patch 64 lost daily-spike resolution; patch 16 hit attention quadratic cost.
  • RoPE vs learned absolute — RoPE +0.3 pp MAPE on long-horizon, identical on short.
  • Continuous vs discrete quantile — identical CRPS, but continuous removed 0.4% of forecasts that had non-monotone quantiles.
  • Trunk size — 200 M vs 500 M. 200 M was within 0.2 pp MAPE on our routes; we ship 200 M.
  • Context length — 2 K vs 16 K. 16 K helped international routes by ~0.6 pp MAPE (multi-year holiday pattern), no help on domestic.

Interview Q&A — TimesFM internals

Walk me through one forward pass.
Per-window normalize, reshape into 32-step patches, residual MLP from 32 to d_model=1280, 20 decoder layers (causal attention with RoPE + QK-norm + per-dim scaling, SwiGLU MLPs), then a continuous-quantile output head that emits 128 future timepoints × 10 quantiles per token. Denormalize at the end. The whole thing is one forward pass for a 30-day forecast.
Why patching?
Three reasons. (1) Quadratic attention — 16 K timepoints become 512 tokens; without patching, attention is unaffordable. (2) Built-in spike filtering — the patch averages over 32 timepoints, so single-day spikes lose attention weight unless they cross patches. (3) Long-horizon output — the asymmetric in/out patches (32/128) let one token emit 128 future steps in one pass.
Why decoder-only and not encoder-decoder like Chronos-T5?
Variable horizon, KV cache reuse, and same loss as language models. Encoder-decoder commits to a fixed output length and recomputes the encoder for each new horizon extension. For our 30-day-and-beyond forecasts, decoder-only wins. Chronos-T5 beats us on horizon-1 by ~1–2% MAPE if you tune; we don't care about horizon-1.
What does RoPE do that learned absolute positions don't?
RoPE encodes relative position by rotating query/key vectors. Two consequences: (1) the attention dot product naturally falls off with relative distance, which matches the inductive bias of TS. (2) Position is unbounded — you can run a 32 K context at inference even if you trained on 16 K. Learned absolute positions hard-cap the trained length and degrade past it.
What changed in TimesFM 2.5 vs 2.0?
Smaller (200 M vs 500 M), longer context (16 K vs 2 K), continuous quantile head replacing 10-bin discrete, RoPE + QK-norm position encoding, and the covariate API was restored (after being removed in 2.0). For us the win was: cheaper inference, monotone quantiles, native covariates, fewer post-hoc fixups.
What does QK-norm fix?
Heavy-tailed values explode the QK dot product before softmax, which bounces the attention distribution to a near-one-hot mode and stalls training. QK-norm applies LayerNorm to Q and K before the dot product, bounding the magnitudes. Stabilizes pretraining and lets the model train at a slightly higher learning rate.
If you had to pick one architectural design choice that mattered most, which?
Patching. Without patching, no 16 K context, no native long-horizon output, no cheap inference. Everything else (RoPE, QK-norm, quantile head) is incremental. Patching is the choice that turned a transformer from too-expensive-for-TS into the obvious tool.
What can't this architecture do well?
Hard regime shifts inside the context window (the patching averages them out). Very short series (< 32 timepoints, one patch). Joint multivariate dynamics across series (it's per-series; cross-series signal is in the frozen trunk + LoRA). Counterfactual / causal inference (it's a forecaster). And it can predict negative values; you have to clamp post-hoc.
Why frozen trunk + LoRA, not full fine-tune?
Three measured reasons. (1) Full FT erased cold-start performance (10.2% → 14.7% MAPE on cold-start routes). (2) Compute — $640 vs $7 per quarter retrain. (3) Audit — one signed base + small adapters is one regulated artefact; full FT is 141 of them. The 0.4 pp accuracy gap on fine-tuned routes is worth the governance and cold-start preservation.
Walk me through the continuous quantile head.
A small MLP from d_model to 128 future timepoints × K parameters of a monotone CDF. The CDF is parameterised as a cumulative softplus over learned bin edges, so q10 < q20 < ... < q90 by construction. We sample at the 9 standard quantiles for the JSON contract. Continuous removes the v1/v2 bug where ~0.4% of forecasts had non-monotone quantiles.
How does the model handle covariates?
Through a separate XReg head (post-trunk). The trunk processes the target series as before; the XReg head reads (a) the trunk hidden state at the last patch and (b) covariate vectors for each future timepoint, and emits a residual correction. v2.5 ships this natively; in v2.0 we built our own XReg head externally. See finetuning.html for our XReg details.
If you had unlimited budget, what would you change?
Add a hierarchical-reconciliation head so route forecasts coherently sum to network totals (we currently reconcile post-hoc with MinT — see hierarchical-forecasting.html). And: full mixture-of-experts variant (Moirai-MoE proved this is the right scaling axis). 200 M dense vs 1 B sparse-MoE is the next bake-off.
