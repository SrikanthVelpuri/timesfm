Evaluation metrics for probabilistic forecasts

The C++ bid-price optimizer does not consume a point forecast. It consumes a distribution. So our evaluation has to be about distribution quality, not just median accuracy. This page is the metric stack we use, the math behind each metric, and the interview-grade answers for why each one matters.

TL;DR. Track seven metrics in CI: point MAPE, sMAPE, RMSE, pinball loss (per quantile), 80% PI coverage, calibration ECE, and CRPS. The release gate fails on any of: MAPE > 6.0% network, coverage outside [78%, 82%], ECE > 0.04. Calibration is the most important metric for AA — a miscalibrated 80% PI burns more revenue than 1 pp of MAPE.

Why distribution metrics, not just MAPE

The bid-price optimizer reads q10 and q90 to compute a risk-aware reservation level. If our q90 is consistently too tight (true coverage 65% instead of 80%), the optimizer over-protects high fare classes, the call center quotes too high, customers churn to competitors, and we lose revenue we could have captured. The model with the best MAPE is not always the model that makes us the most money.

Mental model: MAPE measures center accuracy. Calibration measures the honesty of the uncertainty. The optimizer trusts the uncertainty. Therefore calibration matters more in production than papers usually credit it for.

1. Pinball loss (the loss we train against)

For quantile τ ∈ (0, 1) and prediction q̂τ:

Lτ(y, q̂τ) = max( τ(y − q̂τ),  (τ − 1)(y − q̂τ) )

For the median (τ = 0.5) this reduces to L = 0.5 · |y − q̂| — just MAE. For τ = 0.9, the loss is 9× heavier on under-predictions than over-predictions, which forces the model to push q90 high enough to cover 90% of actuals.

We train against the average over all 10 quantiles (TimesFM's output) plus a small MSE on the median to keep numerical stability:

Ltrain = (1/10) Στ ∈ {0.1, …, 0.9} Lτ  +  0.5 · (y − q̂0.5)2

What pinball loss does NOT do

It does not enforce q̂0.1 < q̂0.2 < … < q̂0.9. Each quantile is trained independently. With small batches you sometimes see q40 > q50 in regions of high uncertainty — harmless on a chart, fatal for an optimizer that assumes monotonicity. Hence fix_quantile_crossing=True in the ForecastConfig.

2. Coverage and the 80% PI

Define empirical coverage at level α = 80% as the fraction of held-out actuals that fall between q10 and q90:

cov80% = (1/N) Σi=1N 1[ q̂0.1(i) ≤ y(i) ≤ q̂0.9(i) ]

Target: 80%. Anything in [78%, 82%] is acceptable; outside that we have a problem. For AA we measure coverage:

  • Network-wide on the val cohort.
  • Per route family.
  • On Q4 only (holiday cohort — calibration usually breaks here).
  • On weekend vs weekday.
  • On weather-irrops days (small but high-variance cohort).

Why coverage drifts in production

Three drivers, in order of frequency:

  1. Distribution shift in covariates. A new fuel-price regime that the model wasn't trained on. Coverage typically drops 3–5 pp.
  2. Stale calibration after fine-tune. Re-training Stage A while keeping the temperature scaling unchanged. Coverage drift up to 8 pp.
  3. Upstream feature freshness loss. Competitor fare feed missed an update; the covariate is wrong; the conditional forecast is wrong. Coverage symptom is regional — only routes touched by the bad feature drift.

3. Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

Coverage at one level (80%) is necessary but not sufficient. A model could pass 80% coverage but be miscalibrated at 60% or 90%. ECE summarizes calibration across all quantile levels:

ECE = Στ ∈ bins | empirical_coverage(τ) − τ |

Bins: typically {0.1, 0.2, …, 0.9}. Compute empirical coverage at each level (fraction of actuals below q̂τ), take absolute deviation from τ, sum. Lower is better; ECE = 0 means perfect calibration.

Visual: the reliability diagram. X-axis = nominal quantile level. Y-axis = empirical coverage. A perfect model is the y=x diagonal. Real models curve.

Our release gate: ECE ≤ 0.04 across the 9 bins.

4. CRPS — the full-distribution metric

Continuous Ranked Probability Score generalizes MAE to distributions. For a forecast distribution F with CDF and an actual y:

CRPS(F, y) = ∫−∞∞ ( F(x) − 1[x ≥ y] )2 dx

For the discrete-quantile output of TimesFM (10 quantiles), we approximate via:

CRPS ≈ (2/K) Σk=1K Lτk(y, q̂τk)

i.e., the average pinball loss across quantiles is a Monte-Carlo estimate of CRPS. So we already optimize CRPS implicitly during training. We also report it on held-out data because it's a single scalar that captures distribution quality and is easier to communicate than a 9-bin reliability diagram.

5. MAPE, sMAPE, RMSE — point metrics for completeness

MAPE = mean(|y - Ŷ| / |y|)

  • Easy to communicate ("our forecast is off by 5%").
  • Comparable across routes with different absolute fare levels.
  • Standard in the airline industry; required for stakeholder reports.

MAPE pitfalls

  • Asymmetric: penalizes over-prediction more than under-prediction.
  • Undefined for y=0 (not an issue for fares; matters for demand).
  • Poor on highly variable series; switch to sMAPE.

sMAPE: 200 · |y − Ŷ| / (|y| + |Ŷ|). Bounded in [0, 200], symmetric — preferred for cross-cohort comparison.

RMSE: penalizes outliers more than MAE. Useful as a sanity check — a model with similar MAPE but much higher RMSE is making big errors on rare days (often the holiday days RMS cares most about).

The eval harness, in code

import numpy as np

def evaluate(model, dataset, horizon=30):
    """Returns the metrics dict gated in CI."""
    point, q = model.forecast_with_covariates(
        inputs=dataset["inputs"],
        dynamic_numerical_covariates=dataset["dyn_num"],
        dynamic_categorical_covariates=dataset["dyn_cat"],
        static_categorical_covariates=dataset["static_cat"],
    )

    y     = np.asarray(dataset["actuals"])
    yhat  = point                      # median
    q10   = q[..., 1]
    q90   = q[..., 9]

    mape  = np.mean(np.abs(y - yhat) / np.abs(y)) * 100
    smape = np.mean(2 * np.abs(y - yhat) / (np.abs(y) + np.abs(yhat))) * 100
    rmse  = np.sqrt(np.mean((y - yhat) ** 2))

    cov80 = np.mean((y >= q10) & (y <= q90)) * 100

    # ECE across 9 quantile levels
    levels = np.arange(1, 10) / 10.0   # 0.1, 0.2, ..., 0.9
    ece = 0.0
    for k, tau in enumerate(levels):
        empirical = np.mean(y <= q[..., k+1])
        ece += abs(empirical - tau)

    # CRPS approximation
    crps = 0.0
    for k, tau in enumerate(levels):
        diff = y - q[..., k+1]
        crps += np.mean(np.maximum(tau * diff, (tau - 1) * diff))
    crps *= 2.0 / len(levels)

    return {
        "mape": mape,
        "smape": smape,
        "rmse": rmse,
        "coverage_80pct": cov80,
        "ece": ece,
        "crps": crps,
    }

Conformal prediction — the alternative we did not ship

Conformal prediction is a model-agnostic post-hoc calibration method. Given a calibrated score distribution from a held-out set, it produces prediction intervals with provable finite-sample coverage guarantees. Sounds great for a regulated environment like RMS. Why we did not use it:

  1. TimesFM's built-in quantile head, with our pinball-loss training, gave coverage in [78%, 82%] without any post-hoc adjustment. The provable guarantee was not worth the engineering.
  2. Conformal intervals are uniformly wide on conditional intervals — the same margin around any conditional median — unless we use conditional conformal, which is more complex and undermines the “simple guarantee” appeal.
  3. We'd still need to monitor coverage in production. Conformal ≠ "set and forget".

If we had a black-box external forecaster we couldn't modify, conformal would be the right choice. For TimesFM where we control the loss function, training pinball directly is more efficient.

Calibration drift in the wild

Three real production stories from the project (with anonymized cohorts):

  • October 2025: coverage on Caribbean leisure family dropped from 79% to 71% over 4 days. Root cause: ATPCO competitor fare feed was 18 hours stale due to upstream Sabre incident. Resolution: marked the feed unhealthy, fell back to last-known competitor fare, coverage recovered in 1 day.
  • Stage 4 release candidate: in CI, coverage on holiday-day cohort was 73%. The Q4 cohort had insufficient holiday samples in train; the LoRA had over-fit on weekday patterns. Resolution: stratified the training sampler to over-weight Q4 days; coverage on holiday cohort hit 81%.
  • BF16 transition: coverage drifted 0.4 pp when we moved from FP32 to BF16. Below the gate threshold, but spotted by alert. Root cause: FP32 XReg head receiving BF16 inputs; precision loss at the boundary. Resolution: re-trained head with autocast on. Drift recovered.

What metrics I would add if I had more time

  • Quantile loss elasticity: sensitivity of quantile-level loss to small changes in fare. Used in optimizer for second-order risk.
  • Energy score: generalization of CRPS to multivariate forecasts. Useful when AA wants joint demand+fare forecasts.
  • Realized vs forecast distribution divergence (KS or Wasserstein): a single number summarizing how far the realized distribution is from the forecast. Easier to alert on than ECE.

Interview Q&A

Walk me through the math of pinball loss and why it is the right loss for quantile forecasting.
For quantile τ and prediction q̂, pinball is L(y, q̂) = max(τ(y − q̂), (τ − 1)(y − q̂)). Penalizes under-prediction by τ and over-prediction by 1−τ. Minimizing it (in expectation) yields the τ-th quantile of the conditional distribution. This is the Koenker-Bassett result — the foundational paper of quantile regression. For τ=0.9 we punish under-prediction 9× more, forcing the model to push q90 high enough.
What is calibration, and why is it not the same as accuracy?
A model is calibrated if its predicted probabilities match empirical frequencies. A weather model that says "70% chance of rain" is calibrated if 70% of those days actually have rain. Accuracy is about the median; calibration is about the entire predictive distribution. A model can have great median accuracy and terrible calibration — and that's actively dangerous in production because downstream systems trust the uncertainty estimates.
If your 80% PI coverage drops to 70%, what is your debugging procedure?
Three checks in order. (1) Did the input distribution shift? Compare summary stats of the last 7 days vs the training distribution. (2) Is the issue cohort-specific? Slice coverage by route family, season, day-of-week — is the drop concentrated? (3) Did an upstream feature go stale? Check the pipeline lineage; if the competitor fare feed missed updates, the conditional forecast is feeding off bad data. Most production drops are in category 3.
Why CRPS over Brier or NLL?
CRPS handles continuous targets natively (fares); Brier is for binary outcomes; NLL requires assuming a parametric distribution. CRPS reduces to MAE for point forecasts, which means it's comparable across point and probabilistic models — a useful property for benchmarking. NLL is fine for parametric models like DeepAR, but TimesFM produces non-parametric quantile output, so NLL is undefined.
When would you prefer conformal prediction over a learned quantile head?
When (a) the underlying model is a black box you can't retrain, (b) you need provable finite-sample coverage for a regulator, or (c) you have a very small calibration set where a learned head would over-fit. For all three, conformal's nonparametric guarantee shines. For our case — TimesFM with a learned quantile head trained on millions of windows — conformal's benefits don't outweigh its uniform-width limitation.
How do you handle the trade-off between sharpness and coverage?
Strictly proper scoring rules force you to confront it. Pinball loss penalizes both miscalibration and unnecessary width — a wider PI hurts when the actual lands inside, a tighter PI hurts when actuals fall outside. Optimizing pinball gives you the right Pareto trade-off automatically. The temptation is to manually widen PIs to "guarantee coverage", but that increases pinball and the optimizer downstream over-protects revenue. Trust the loss.
What's the relationship between pinball loss, CRPS, and quantile crossing?
Pinball is a per-quantile loss. CRPS approximates by averaging pinball over quantile levels. Neither enforces ordering — you can train each quantile head independently and end up with q40 > q50. The fix is either a constrained loss (penalty term for crossings) or a post-hoc projection (sort the quantiles after prediction, our approach via fix_quantile_crossing=True). The latter is mathematically equivalent to the isotonic-regression projection to the simplex of monotonic quantiles.
