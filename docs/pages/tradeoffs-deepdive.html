Tradeoffs deep-dive — every decision and what we gave up

This page is the cross-cutting summary of design decisions across the entire stack — model choice, fine-tuning, inference, evaluation, deployment, monitoring — with explicit tradeoffs called out. Read it as a flat list of “we chose X over Y because Z, and gave up A” statements. The individual decision pages have the math; this page has the rationale.

TL;DR. 18 cross-cutting decisions, each with a chosen path, the runner-up that almost won, and a one-paragraph rationale. The pattern: prefer cheaper, more reversible decisions; pay for accuracy only when revenue attribution justifies it; keep the operational surface small.

1. Foundation model vs per-route classical

Chose: TimesFM 2.5 (200M-param decoder transformer, 100B time-series points pretrain).
Runner-up: per-route ARIMAX with hierarchical pooling.

Pros of foundation model

  • Cold-start on new routes works in zero-shot.
  • Cross-route information transfer is automatic.
  • One trunk + 17 LoRAs replaces 6,700 ARIMAX models.
  • Better calibration on long-horizon quantiles (CRPS ↓ 33%).

What we gave up

  • ~$200/quarter base inference cost vs ~$50 for ARIMAX (still trivial).
  • Lose interpretability of AR coefficients (SHAP-by-covariate replaces).
  • Harder for an analyst to debug a single weird route forecast.

2. TimesFM 2.5 vs Chronos-Bolt vs Lag-Llama

Chose: TimesFM 2.5 for the larger pretrain corpus and continuous quantile head.
Runner-up: Chronos-Bolt-Base (very close on accuracy, 12% slower at inference).

The decisive factor was the use_continuous_quantile_head behaviour for our 30-day horizon: TimesFM's long-horizon calibration was 8% better on the held-out intl-long cohort. Chronos was 0.8 pp behind on network MAPE and would have required custom logic to enforce monotonic quantiles for the optimizer.

3. PEFT (LoRA) vs full fine-tune vs continued pretraining

Chose: two-stage PEFT (XReg head + rank-8 LoRA per route family).
Rejected: full fine-tune at $45K/quarter for 0.4 pp MAPE; continued pretraining at $12K/quarter with no measurable gain.

Trade-off accepted. 0.4 pp MAPE gap to full fine-tune translates to roughly $4M of revenue lift left on the table. We chose the $44.99K cost saving and a more composable artifact. Re-evaluate if AA secures pretraining budget.

4. LoRA rank 8 vs 4 vs 16

Chose: rank 8 by default; rank 16 for Asia long-haul only.

Rank 4 left 0.3 pp MAPE on the table for half the cost — not worth it. Rank 16 caught one additional 0.1 pp on most families, mostly noise, but on Asia long-haul it was 0.4 pp of real gain because Asia has more idiosyncrasy (currency cycles, two-hemisphere holidays). We let one family run at rank 16 and the rest at rank 8.

5. Per-route adapters vs route-family adapters

Chose: route-family adapters (20 families covering 141 OD-pairs).
Rejected: 141 per-route adapters.

Per-route adapters would overfit on low-volume long-haul routes inside half an epoch and would create 141 rollback decisions per quarter. Route families give each adapter 50K–200K windows, share a single PI calibration, and collapse on-call surface to 20 decisions. Cost: ~0.3 pp on the most idiosyncratic OD-pair.

6. XReg head architecture — MLP vs transformer-cross-attn

Chose: shallow MLP (2 layers, 1280 → 512 → 10 quantiles).
Considered: cross-attention block conditioning the trunk output on covariate embeddings.

The shallow MLP captures additive covariate effects, which is what AA pricing actually is. Cross-attention introduced multiplicative interactions we couldn't justify operationally and added 800K params. We'd come back to it if elasticity coefficients show second-order terms (they don't today).

7. Pinball loss vs Gaussian NLL vs CRPS

Chose: pinball loss across 10 quantiles + median MSE.
Rejected: Gaussian NLL (parametric assumption invalid — fares are skewed); CRPS (computationally expensive across 30-day horizon).

Pinball is the right loss when your downstream consumer wants quantiles — which our bid-price optimizer does. Gaussian NLL would have been wrong for premium fares; CRPS would have been correct but ~3× the training cost.

8. 90-day context vs 180 vs 360

Chose: 90-day context for nightly batch, 180-day for off-cycle re-score.

90 days captures one full quarter of weekly + monthly seasonality and fits the patch structure (3 patches of 32 + tail). 180 added 0.2 pp accuracy at 3× the inference cost; we use it only for off-cycle re-score where latency budget is more relaxed. 360 added nothing beyond 180 because we'd retrained the LoRA on quarterly cadence anyway, capturing the longer trend implicitly.

9. 30-day horizon vs 14 vs 60

Chose: 30-day horizon to match the bid-price optimizer's decision window.

The C++ optimizer makes pricing decisions on a 30-day forward window. Forecasting further is wasted work; forecasting shorter forces the optimizer to extrapolate. We aligned the model output to the consumer's decision horizon. A separate 90-day forecast feeds long-range network planning, but uses different aggregation (weekly, not daily).

10. Batch=64 vs 32 vs 128

Chose: batch=64 for nightly inference on A10G.

Batch=32 left 30% throughput on the table; batch=128 spilled to global memory and dropped throughput by 8%. 64 is the A10G sweet spot for our patch structure. Tuned once with a profiler; revisit if hardware changes.

11. BF16 vs FP16 vs INT8

Chose: BF16 for inference; FP32 for training.
Rejected: INT8 dynamic quantisation (PI coverage dropped to 73%); FP16 (overflow risk on premium-cabin fares >$5K).

BF16's wider exponent range matters because we have fares from $80 to $11,000 and we don't want premium-cabin tail forecasts to overflow. INT8's precision hit was visible directly in the q90/q10 spread — bid-price-grade quantiles couldn't take it.

12. torch.compile vs ONNX/TensorRT

Chose: torch.compile with reduced-overhead mode.
Rejected: ONNX + TensorRT.

TensorRT had a 5% kernel-coverage gap that fell back to CUDA, eliminating its advantage. torch.compile sat us at 6 ms with one line of code and one rollback path: drop the decorator. Operational complexity ratio decided this.

13. LoRA hot-swap protocol — pinned memory vs disk-load

Chose: pinned-memory state-dict updates with a per-family LRU cache.

Disk-load was 800 ms per swap; pinned-memory hot-swap is 8 ms. The 100× gain matters because nightly inference touches all 17 adapters in the 41-second window. We cache all 17 adapters in pinned memory throughout the run; total memory cost is ~80 MB, which is rounding error on an A10G.

14. Quantile crossing prevention

Chose: fix_quantile_crossing=True at inference; pinball training without monotonic constraint.

Pinball loss does not enforce monotonicity. About 0.5% of horizon predictions had q60 > q70 in raw output. The bid-price optimizer requires monotonic quantiles (it does inverse CDF lookups). Inference-time sort is a 12-line patch and resolves the issue without affecting accuracy.

15. Quarterly retraining vs continuous learning

Chose: quarterly XReg refit + on-demand LoRA refit per family.
Rejected: continuous online learning.

Continuous learning makes rollback hard, makes A/B difficult, and makes auditability impossible. We'd be unable to answer “why did fares move yesterday?” if the model itself was moving daily. Quarterly cadence is right for the regime-shift frequency in airline pricing; on-demand is right for one-off shocks.

16. Per-route ARIMAX as fallback vs no fallback

Chose: shadow-mode ARIMAX runs in parallel for canary + first 6 weeks; head-only forecast as runtime fallback.

Shadow ARIMAX gave us “known-good” comparison during canary; we retired it at week 6 once the foundation model held SLOs. At runtime, head-only forecast (no LoRA) is the fallback if an adapter fails to load — degrades gracefully to ~6.0% MAPE instead of returning an error. We chose this over “serve last good forecast” because pricing decisions need to reflect today's covariates, not yesterday's.

17. Bid-price optimizer ownership — ours vs separate team

Chose: separate team owns the optimizer; we own the forecast.

Antitrust separation. Forecast is a probabilistic statement about future demand; pricing policy is a business decision. Mixing them in one codebase makes legal review harder and creates a feedback loop (model trains on its own past prices). The contract between teams is a JSON spec for the q10/q50/q90 forecast and a separate weekly elasticity refit owned by Operations Research.

18. Azure ML registry vs S3 + Manifest

Chose: Azure ML registry with cosign signatures pinned to training-data hash.
Rejected: S3 + manifest file (less governance integration).

AA is an Azure-first shop. The Azure ML registry gives us SBOM, signature verification, and Microsoft Entra ID for access control out of the box. The cost was 2 days of integration work; the benefit is a single source of truth for “what model is in prod” that audit can query.

The decision framework I used

Every one of these 18 decisions ran through the same three questions. They're worth writing down because they're portable to any ML project:

  1. What does it cost if I'm wrong? Reversible decisions get a quick yes; irreversible decisions need triple-checking. torch.compile is reversible (one line). LoRA architecture choice is reversible (retrain takes 6 hours). Quantile head architecture is much harder to reverse (it's downstream of every artifact).
  2. What does it cost if I'm right but a year late? Timeline cost matters. We chose torch.compile over TensorRT partly because the time-to-ship was a week vs a month. The 0.5% potential extra speedup wasn't worth a month of rollout delay.
  3. Can the on-call engineer fix it at 2 a.m.? If a decision creates a system that requires me-specifically to debug, that's an operational tax. Pinned-memory hot-swap is one Python file; ONNX + TensorRT is a toolchain. We chose the former.
Tradeoffs aren’t about being clever. They’re about being honest with yourself about what you’re willing to give up — and naming it explicitly so future-you, or your replacement, can revisit the decision when the constraints change.
