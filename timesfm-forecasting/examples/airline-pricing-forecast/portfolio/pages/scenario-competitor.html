Scenario 3 — The competitor undercut

At 06:14 a.m. CST, Delta drops main-cabin fares on Atlanta-LAX by $20. The AA response has to reach customer-facing channels within ~30 minutes or the substitution effect bleeds bookings to Delta. The model + off-cycle re-score path closes that loop in about 14 minutes p50.

TL;DR. Sabre fare-shop ingestion picks up Delta's move at 06:18. The competitor-fare covariate updates for AA's adjacent routes (DFW-LAX, ORD-LAX, MIA-LAX). Off-cycle re-score fires at 06:21, completes 06:28. Forecast adjustments: -$11 on hub-corp routes (0.55 elasticity), -$17 on event-leisure routes (0.85). The optimizer reads the new forecast and matches Delta within $3 on leisure routes; leaves hub-corp pricing unchanged. Customer-facing fare update: 06:32 CST, 18 minutes after Delta's move.

Trigger

Delta files new fares for Atlanta-LAX in ATPCO at 06:14:08 a.m. CST. AA's Sabre fare-shop ingestion polls every 4 minutes, picks up the new fares at 06:17:52. The delta is large enough to trigger the off-cycle re-score path: >$8 fare change on a matching-cluster route.

Covariate inputs

Only the competitor_fare array changes for the affected routes. Every other covariate is unchanged.

Model behavior

The XReg head's competitor-elasticity coefficient is family-specific:

FamilyElasticityForecast adjustment for $20 competitor drop
AA-DFW-LAX (hub-corp)0.55-$11.00
AA-MIA-LAX (leisure-hub)0.70-$14.00
AA-ORD-LAX (event-leisure-ish)0.85-$17.00
AA-ATL-LAX (direct overlap)0.90-$18.00

The model does NOT automatically match. It produces a conditional expected fare given that the competitor's fare is now lower. The optimizer decides whether to match.

Important separation: the model produces forecast distributions; the optimizer applies pricing policy. Keeping these separate means the forecast is audit-defensible (it's a prediction, not a price-setting action) and the policy can change without retraining the model.

Optimizer response

The optimizer's policy on competitor matching is family-specific:

  • Hub-corp (DFW-LAX, ORD-LAX): Match conservatively. AA loyalty (AAdvantage, corp contracts) buffers substitution. Match within $3 of competitor — not exactly.
  • Leisure-hub (MIA-LAX): Match within $5. Some loyalty cushion but customers are price-sensitive.
  • Event-leisure (ORD-LAS-style routes, if there were one in this cluster): Match within $1. Almost no loyalty cushion; full match.
  • Direct-overlap (ATL-LAX): Match exactly. Same OD-pair as the competitor; no cushion.

On DFW-LAX, the model says expected fare drops to ~$294 (from $305). The optimizer chooses a bid-price aligned to ~$300 (Delta + $15) — aggressive enough to capture AAdvantage demand, conservative enough not to undershoot AA's own positioning.

The 14-minute off-cycle latency

06:14:08  Delta files new ATPCO fares
06:17:52  Sabre fare-shop ingestion picks up the change (4-min poll)
06:18:14  Datadog metric "competitor_fare_delta" flips above threshold
06:18:21  Off-cycle re-score job dispatched
06:18:36  Job lands on the warm GPU instance (already running, no spin-up)
06:18:49  Re-score begins on affected route cluster (54 OD-pairs)
06:19:18  Re-score completes (~30 s for 54 routes vs 41 s for 6,700)
06:19:24  Updated forecasts written to feature store
06:19:31  Optimizer's read cache TTL expires
06:19:42  Optimizer reads new forecast, computes new bid-prices
06:20:08  Bid-prices propagate to Sabre PSS

p50 latency from Delta filing to customer-facing fare: ~14 minutes. p99: ~38 minutes. Pre-TimesFM, this loop took ~2 hours because the per-route ARIMAX re-fits were too slow to do off-cycle — we just waited for the nightly batch.

Trade-offs

Match elasticity: how aggressively to follow competitors

High elasticity (e.g., 0.9 on hub-corp)

  • Maximizes booking capture during competitor moves.
  • Keeps AA visible at the top of fare-comparison sites.

High elasticity (downside)

  • Erodes pricing power on routes where AAdvantage loyalty is real.
  • Triggers price-war dynamics — competitors match back, fares spiral down.
  • Empirically, AA captures ~60% of substitution-eligible demand at 0.55 elasticity, ~75% at 0.9 elasticity — but at 0.9 the absolute fare is lower, so total revenue is roughly flat.

How the elasticity was estimated

Stage A fine-tune fit the elasticity coefficient on 24 months of historical data where competitor moves were observable. The fit is per-family (rank-32 cross term in the XReg head). Sanity-check: the OR team had a long-standing belief that hub-corp elasticity should be 0.5–0.6 from booking-curve analysis; we got 0.55. Strong cross-validation.

Antitrust considerations

AA does NOT explicitly target a "match Delta exactly" policy. The model produces an expected fare given competitor moves; the optimizer applies a conservative buffer. The decoupling is by design — both for defensibility and because explicit matching rules would be visible in the artifacts and could create regulatory risk.

Where this could fail

  1. Sabre fare-shop polling lag. If the polling interval drops or fails, the competitor covariate is stale. Symptom: forecast doesn't move when competitor moves; AA looks slow on aa.com. Mitigation: feature freshness watchdog pages within 30 min of staleness.
  2. Spurious competitor signal (data quality). ATPCO occasionally publishes a temporary fare that's an artifact (filing error). Mitigation: smoothing over a 30-min window; require persistent fare to trigger the threshold.
  3. Cluster definition wrong. If we treat ATL-LAX as not adjacent to DFW-LAX (different cluster), we miss the substitution. Cluster definitions are reviewed quarterly by Pricing.

Real-world variants

  • United price increase. Same logic, opposite direction. Forecast lifts +$11 on adjacent hub-corp routes; optimizer decides whether to follow or maintain price advantage.
  • Spirit / Frontier ULCC undercut. Lower-cost carriers; their prices are systematically below AA's. We track them but the elasticity is lower (~0.2 on hub routes) because the customer segments overlap less.
  • International codeshare partner. If British Airways (a partner) drops a transatlantic fare, AA usually follows because of the joint-venture revenue split; elasticity ~0.95 on those routes.
  • Multi-lateral move. All major US carriers raise fares in the same morning (typical of fuel-surcharge announcements). Each carrier reads the others' moves; the model handles each pairwise interaction independently.

Interview Q&A

Why is the competitor elasticity coefficient family-specific?
Customer segments differ. Hub-corp routes (DFW-LAX) have a high share of business travelers on AAdvantage status / corporate contracts who are insensitive to competitor moves — price difference matters less than schedule and lounge access. Leisure-event routes (ORD-LAS) have price-sensitive vacationers who substitute easily. The model captures this through the rank-32 cross-route interaction term in the XReg head.
How would you detect if a competitor were trying to game the system?
Two patterns to watch. (1) Spike-and-revert: competitor files a low fare for 5 minutes then withdraws it, hoping to bait AA into a long-running match. Defense: require persistence (covariate is updated only after fares hold for >15 min). (2) Tit-for-tat oscillation: each carrier matches the other's last-known fare, fares spiral down. Defense: optimizer's match buffer ($3 on hub-corp) breaks the symmetric spiral.
Could two carriers' models “collude” emergently without explicit coordination?
Theoretically possible if both trained on similar data and use similar elasticity assumptions; in practice the noise in real demand and the differing elasticities across families prevent equilibrium convergence. We monitor for it — if AA's and Delta's fares move in lock-step on a route for >2 weeks with no underlying demand reason, that's a signal to re-examine the elasticity coefficient and document for compliance.
What if Sabre's fare-shop polling fails for an hour?
Feature freshness watchdog pages the data team within 30 min of staleness. The forecast falls back to the last-known competitor fare; the optimizer keeps using the last-known bid-prices. Customer-facing fares stay where they were — might be over- or under-priced relative to the now-current competitor, but the cost is bounded (you'd lose at most an hour of bookings to mispricing). Better than firing the optimizer with a NaN covariate.
Why don't you just match the competitor exactly on every route?
Two reasons. (1) Loyalty math: ~28% of AA hub-corp bookings come from AAdvantage status / corp contracts; they'll pay an AA premium up to ~$30 for status credit and lounge access. Matching exactly leaves $30 of revenue on the table. (2) Antitrust risk: an explicit "match exactly" policy is more visible than a probabilistic match-with-elasticity rule, and creates audit risk.
