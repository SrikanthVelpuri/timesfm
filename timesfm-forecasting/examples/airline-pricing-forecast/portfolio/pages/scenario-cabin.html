Scenario 6 — Cabin upsell forecast

Strategy team asks: "What would happen if we converted 30 economy seats on DFW-LAX to premium economy starting next month?" RMS needs a fare forecast for the premium economy cabin specifically, not just main cabin. Same model, different static covariate, different forecast distribution.

TL;DR. Flip the static cabin covariate from main to premium-economy; keep all other covariates the same. The model produces a forecast median ~2.6× the main-cabin median ($960 vs $370 on DFW-LAX) and a PI 4× wider ($600 vs $135). The wider PI is structurally correct: premium-economy demand is more idiosyncratic. Optimizer treats premium economy as a separate fare-class ladder. Cabin-mix simulation: same nightly pipeline, same model, just an extra static label per scenario.

Trigger

Strategy team is evaluating a cabin reconfiguration. They want to model the revenue impact of converting 30 main-cabin seats to premium economy on DFW-LAX. The question is two-part:

  1. What fares can we expect on the new premium-economy seats?
  2. Will the displaced main-cabin demand cannibalize main-cabin fares (do bookings spill into Y class, vs go to a competitor)?

The model handles question 1 cleanly. Question 2 is more complex and combines the forecast with a separate demand-spillover model maintained by the OR team.

Covariate inputs

Only the static cabin covariate changes. The historical context is different too — we use existing premium-economy fare history on DFW-LAX rather than main-cabin history.

The PI is much wider on premium economy. Three reasons:

  1. Smaller sample. Premium economy has ~30 seats vs ~150 main; daily fare averages are noisier.
  2. Idiosyncratic buyers. Corporate executives, last-minute upgrades, schedule-driven upsells. Less predictable demand process than vacation main-cabin.
  3. Wider price discrimination. Premium economy ladder has ~6 fare classes vs ~10 in main; smaller increments mean larger fare jumps within a single day.
Wider PI is correct. A model that pretended premium-economy fares were as predictable as main-cabin fares would systematically over-claim certainty. The optimizer would close inventory too aggressively at the q90, missing the last-minute upsell upside. Calibrated wide PIs let the optimizer protect more high-yield seats for the upsell that actually materializes 30% of the time.

The cabin-cabin interaction

The XReg head has a rank-32 cross-route interaction term that learns interactions between static and dynamic covariates. For cabin specifically:

InteractionEffect
cabin == premium × holiday_flag == 2+$290 on premium-economy holiday days (vs +$95 main)
cabin == premium × day_of_week == Sun+$0 (no Sunday tilt for premium; main has +$45 leisure-return lift)
cabin == premium × competitor_fare delta0.10 elasticity (vs 0.55 main-cabin) — premium customers don't comparison-shop
cabin == premium × days_to_departure-$3.2 / day (vs -$1.4 main) — faster price escalation as departure nears

These interactions emerge from the rank-32 cross term, not hand-coded. The Stage-A fine-tune learned them from historical data. Sanity check: the OR team independently flagged that premium-economy holiday lift should be ~3× main-cabin lift; we got 3.05×. Good cross-validation.

Simulation: 30 main-cabin seats → premium-economy

The simulation requires more than just running the model under different cabin labels. The OR team combines the model output with a demand-spillover model:

  1. Forecast premium-economy fares on the affected dates (this page's output).
  2. Estimate premium-economy demand at those fares (separate model, not in scope).
  3. Compute the resulting load factor; if <90%, the seats are imperfect substitutes.
  4. Estimate displaced main-cabin bookings and their landing class (Y? Q? competitor?).
  5. Net revenue: premium-economy capture - main-cabin spillover loss.

Strategy did this for several months of forward bookings; the simulation said the cabin reconfiguration was net-positive ~$2.4M annual revenue. The forecast model contributed step 1 (and partial step 4 via main-cabin q90).

Trade-offs

Single model with cabin covariate vs cabin-specific models

Single model + cabin covariate (chosen)

  • Same training pipeline, same artifact registry.
  • Cross-cabin interactions learned via the rank-32 term.
  • New cabin types ship by adding a static label, no new model.
  • Cabin-mix simulation runs the same nightly pipeline.

Cabin-specific models (rejected)

  • Each cabin type is a separate artifact; multiplies registry size.
  • No native cabin-cross interactions; need a separate joint model.
  • Per-cabin fine-tune compute — doubles or triples the bill.
  • Operationally complex.

Where this could fail

  1. Cabin reconfiguration changes the implicit cabin meaning. If “premium economy” on a 787 means something different from “premium economy” on a narrowbody (different seat pitch, different amenities), the static label is mis-applied. Mitigation: separate static labels for premium-economy-narrow vs premium-economy-wide.
  2. Insufficient premium-economy history. If a route has only 3 months of premium-economy operation, the context is short and PI inflates significantly. Mitigation: borrow from analog routes (same approach as cold-start).
  3. Spillover model is wrong. The fare forecast is right but the OR demand-spillover model is wrong, leading to a bad simulation conclusion. The forecast model isn't responsible for this; the chain of models is.

Real-world variants

  • Business class on transatlantic. Static covariate cabin == business, much wider PI ($1,200–$1,500), more pronounced holiday and DTD effects.
  • First class on transcon (Flagship First). Even wider PI; near-zero competitor elasticity.
  • Bundling change (basic-economy introduction). A new cabin label, requires retraining Stage A to learn its coefficients. Not a simulation, an actual model change.
  • Premium-cabin de-bundling. Strategy considered selling premium-economy seats with food/wifi separated. Different demand process; would need its own static label for “premium-economy basic.”

Interview Q&A

Why is the PI on premium economy 4× wider than main cabin?
Three drivers. (1) Smaller sample — ~30 seats per flight vs ~150, so the daily average fare is a noisier estimate. (2) Customer mix — corporate executives and last-minute upsells are less predictable than vacationing main-cabin buyers. (3) Wider price discrimination — premium economy has ~6 fare classes spanning a $700 range vs main with ~10 classes spanning $200. The model's quantile head learned this from history. The wider PI is calibrated and correct.
Why one model with a cabin covariate instead of a separate model per cabin?
Three reasons. (1) Cross-cabin interactions are real — main-cabin holiday lift correlates with premium-economy holiday lift; a joint model learns this. (2) Operational simplicity — one artifact, one CI gate, one rollback path. (3) New cabin types don't require new models — just a new static label and a coefficient learned by Stage A. Cabin-specific models would double or triple the registry without payback.
Walk me through the cabin-mix simulation end-to-end.
(1) Run the forecast model with cabin = premium-economy on the affected dates. (2) Hand the output to the OR team's demand-spillover model, which estimates booking volume at the forecasted fares. (3) Compute load factor; if <90%, model the displaced main-cabin demand. (4) Run the model again with cabin = main on the affected dates with reduced inventory; estimate spillover bookings landing in Y class or going to competitor. (5) Net revenue = premium-economy capture - main-cabin loss. The forecast model is one of three components; spillover and demand are separate models.
What would change if AA introduced basic economy as a new cabin?
Basic economy is a separate cabin label. It would require retraining Stage A to learn its coefficient (probably negative relative to main cabin, with weaker DTD effect because basic economy is bought early by leisure customers). This is a model change, not a simulation. The first 30 days would use the cold-start synthetic-prior approach with main-cabin as the analog, then taper as real basic-economy history accumulates.
How does the rank-32 cross term in the XReg head work mathematically?
It's a low-rank approximation of an interaction matrix. With 11 covariates, a full second-order interaction is 11×11 = 121 terms. Some of those interactions matter (cabin×holiday) and most don't. A rank-32 factorization parameterizes the interaction matrix as U VT with U, V ∈ ℝ11×32; we have a 32-dim hidden space for interactions. Empirically rank 32 captures all the interactions we've verified (cabin×holiday, cabin×DTD, etc.) and saturates — rank 64 gave no additional MAPE improvement.
