Scenario 4 — The weather irrops

A winter storm warning is issued for Chicago O'Hare for Tuesday and Wednesday. Operations sets the weather_disruption covariate to 1 for any route segment touching ORD on those days. Forecast adjusts -$22; optimizer dumps low-class inventory; better $180 in the seat than no-show on the day.

TL;DR. Weather covariate flips from 0 to 1 on the affected days. XReg coefficient is -$22 (a learned average of historical irrops fare suppression). The forecast median drops only on the affected days; surrounding days unaffected. The PI widens too because irrops days have higher variance (re-accommodation, last-minute cancellations, refunds). The biggest design choice: train on the scheduled weather flag, not the realized outcome — this prevents a feedback loop where dumping inventory makes irrops days look even cheaper in the next training cycle.

Trigger

NWS issues a winter-storm warning for ORD covering Tuesday 06:00 through Wednesday 18:00. Ops dashboards flip; the AA Network Operations Center decides whether to issue a Ground Delay Program. Either way, the weather_disruption covariate gets set to 1 on D+1 and D+2 for routes with ORD origin or destination.

Covariate inputs

The weather forecast feed (NOAA + private vendor blend) updates every 6 hours. The next nightly batch picks up the new flags. For high-magnitude warnings, an off-cycle re-score fires immediately rather than waiting for the nightly.

Note the asymmetric PI move: q10 drops more than q90. The model learned that irrops days have a longer left-tail — some passengers are re-accommodated at the originally-paid fare; some are bumped to revenue flights at last-seat prices; a fraction are refunded entirely. The downside variance is bigger than the upside.

Why we train on scheduled, not realized, weather

Critical design choice. If we train the XReg coefficient on realized irrops days (i.e., days where actual cancellations happened), we create a feedback loop: lower forecasts → lower bid-prices → cheaper seats sold → lower realized fares → bigger negative coefficient on retrain. The coefficient amplifies over each training cycle.

Instead, we train on the scheduled weather flag — the prediction made the day before. This way the coefficient measures the historical expected effect of predicted irrops, and the feedback loop is broken: a forecasted storm that doesn't actually materialize does not retroactively change training data.

Where this could fail

  1. Forecasted storm misses. NWS predicts ground delay; storm tracks south. AA dropped fares for nothing. Operationally OK because customers already bought at the lower fare; we just pocketed less revenue. Mitigation: re-evaluate at each NOAA refresh; if forecast retracts, back out the weather flag.
  2. Cascading cancellations. A 2-day storm causes 5 days of schedule recovery. The covariate as designed only covers the storm days. The recovery days are under-modeled. Improvement TODO: extend covariate to capture “recovery period” with separate coefficient.
  3. Hurricane track uncertainty. 5-day forecast cone for a hurricane covers many ORD-bound routes. Covariate flag is binary, so we either flag everything (over-suppress fares) or wait for the cone to narrow (lose the response window). Possible improvement: probabilistic weather covariate with confidence weighting.

Trade-offs

How aggressively to dump inventory

Aggressive dump (-$30+ on irrops days)

  • Fills seats that would otherwise no-show.
  • Reduces customer-care load (refund processing).
  • Better passenger sentiment vs cancelling.

Aggressive dump (downside)

  • If storm doesn't materialize, we sold seats at $230 that would have gone for $310.
  • Sets precedent for “weather discounts” that customers learn to game.

Why the model says -$22 and not -$50

The fitted coefficient reflects the historical average of how AA actually re-prices during irrops — conservatively. AA does not dump aggressively because the operational chaos already eats the margin; further fare cuts would compound. The -$22 coefficient is calibrated to reality, not to a theoretical optimum.

Real-world variants

  • Multi-hub disruption. Northeast snowstorm hits ORD, JFK, LGA simultaneously. Cluster of routes flag together. Network re-pricing fires once across the affected cluster.
  • Hurricane evacuation routes. Pre-storm, demand on flights away from the affected city spikes. The weather flag suppresses fares in/out (irrops); the underlying demand surge is in the q90 PI but not modeled explicitly. Possible v2 covariate: “evacuation route”.
  • Recovery period after a major event. 3 days after the storm clears, AA is still flying make-up flights to absorb the backlog. Covariate as shipped doesn't cover this; manual override by Pricing.
  • Wildfire / smoke. Different from snowstorm but similar irrops footprint. NWS feeds them through the same warning system; covariate handles them identically.

Interview Q&A

Walk me through the feedback-loop concern with weather covariates.
If we trained on realized irrops outcomes, the model learns from the suppressed fares we set. Lower forecasts in this cycle → lower bid-prices → cheaper sold inventory → lower realized fares → even more negative coefficient next training. The coefficient diverges. We avoid this by training on scheduled (predicted) weather flags — the fitted relationship is between “day-ahead weather forecast” and “realized fare,” not between “realized irrops” and “realized fare.” Static prediction inputs at training prevent feedback.
Why is the q10 wider on irrops days but q90 not?
Asymmetric historical variance. Some irrops days result in deep fare cuts ($150 below normal); others see only modest cuts ($20). Almost no irrops day has a fare spike — the only direction is down. So the left tail of the predictive distribution is fatter, the right tail isn't. The quantile head learned this asymmetry from training data.
If the forecasted storm doesn't materialize, what happens?
The next NOAA refresh removes the flag. We re-score affected routes; forecast bounces back. But customers who already bought at the suppressed fare have those tickets — we don't retroactively raise prices. The revenue cost of a false-positive irrops flag is bounded to the bookings that happened during the flagged window, typically 10–30% of daily volume on those routes. Acceptable cost vs the downside of underpredicting a real storm.
How would you improve the weather covariate?
Make it probabilistic. Currently binary 0/1. Better: a continuous “disruption probability” from NWS's ensemble forecast. The coefficient becomes -$22 × P(storm). At low confidence (50% chance) we suppress fares half as much — better expected revenue if the storm doesn't hit. Trade-off: more complex to communicate to Ops, harder to set a binary “trigger off-cycle re-score” threshold.
How does this interact with AA's flexible-rebooking waivers?
Indirectly. Waivers reduce no-shows (because customers can rebook freely instead of just absorbing the loss), which means less fare suppression is needed. The coefficient is fitted on data from a period with waivers in effect, so the -$22 already accounts for the typical waiver behavior. If AA changed waiver policy materially, we'd need to retrain — one of the things the OR team flags during quarterly elasticity review.
